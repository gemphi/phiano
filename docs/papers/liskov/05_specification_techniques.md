# The word specified by its operations: six criteria for what a relation may say

*Phient interpreted papers · Liskov series, 5 of 16 · 2026-03-09 · on: "Specification Techniques for Data Abstractions" (Barbara H. Liskov and Stephen N. Zilles, 1975, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering SE-1(1), pp. 7–19; also Proc. International Conference on Reliable Software, ACM SIGPLAN Notices 10(6))*

"In Phient's world, a word (w) is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions (c). The Phical paradigm, X ⇒ Y, drives and composes Phient's Phasor Language Models: Phinet, Phiano, PhiTon. Phical stores no point: a word is 32–64 relations, each a complex phasor on a closed circle with a count; meaning is a side effect of pairs of words moving under a condition."

## 1. Our abstract

In 1975 Barbara Liskov and Stephen Zilles asked a question that sounds administrative and is not: how should one write down what a data abstraction is, so that a program can be proved against the description and so that other programs can use the description as an axiom. Their answer was in two parts. First, six criteria that any specification technique must meet: formality, constructibility, comprehensibility, minimality, wide range of applicability, extensibility. Second, a survey of five techniques, sorted into two families. Two of the techniques describe an abstraction by building a model of it out of some other mathematics. Three describe it implicitly, by saying only how its operations behave toward each other. The paper's worked example is the integer stack, specified five times, and its verdict is that no technique wins on every criterion.

This paper reads that survey as a claim about words. Phient holds that a word is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions. That is the constraint Liskov and Zilles put on a data abstraction almost word for word: the behaviour of the objects can be observed only by applications of the operations. A stack is what push, pop and top do to each other. A word in phinet is what its 64 channels do to other words when a condition selects a channel. The algebraic method they describe, in which an abstraction is a set of equations between compositions of operations, is the form of Phical's X ⇒ Y under c. The criterion of minimality, which says a specification must not leak the representation, is the criterion the byte channel of docs/83 failed, and the criterion the three operators of docs/86 were introduced to restore.

Sections 2 to 5 report the paper in its own terms. Sections 6 to 10 read it into phinet, phiano, phiecs, the quest, the helix and the three operators. Section 11 lists the measurements in PHIENT_FACTS.md that bear on it and is careful to say what has not been measured. Section 12 names the experiment that would test the idea inside phinet and names its control. Section 13 says where the analogy breaks. Section 14 gives the verified facts about the authors and the moment. Section 15 lists what remains. Section 16 lists the sources reached and not reached. Every number about Phient in this paper comes from the facts file and is cited as it stands there. Nothing is claimed for a run that was not made.

## 2. What the work claims, in its terms

The paper opens by saying what it is for. In the authors' words: "The main purposes in writing this paper are to discuss the importance of formal specifications and to survey a number of promising specification techniques." Specifications matter, the paper says, because a program is proved correct against a description, and because a proved program then becomes an axiom that other proofs can use. In the paper's phrasing: "a specification is used in two ways: as a description against which a program is proved correct, and as a set of axioms in the proof of other programs." Specifications also serve construction, since a designer introduces them first, to describe the concepts developed, in a precise and unambiguous way.

The paper then argues about the unit. A specification can be attached to a statement, a procedure, or something larger. Attach it to too small a unit and the description is uninteresting and there are too many of them. The right unit, they claim, is the data abstraction, which they define as a group of related functions or operations that act upon a particular class of objects, with the constraint that the behaviour of the objects can be observed only by applications of the operations. The push-down stack is their canonical case: stack objects, and the operations create, push, pop, top. The implementation that fits this unit is the multi-procedure module, a group of procedures that share a representation and hide it.

Against this unit they set six criteria for any specification technique. Formality: the notation must be mathematically sound and processable by machine. Constructibility: a writer must be able to produce the specification without undue difficulty and be sure it captures the intended concept. Comprehensibility: a trained reader must be able to reconstruct the concept from the specification with little effort, so the specification should be small and lucid. Minimality: the specification must capture the interesting properties and nothing else, in particular no detail of an implementation. Wide range of applicability: the technique must describe many kinds of concept naturally. Extensibility: a small change to the concept should produce a small change to the specification.

The survey is the rest of the paper. Five techniques are examined against the six criteria, with the integer stack as the running example. The conclusion is that no single technique satisfies all six, and that a large program will likely need more than one. The paper also says plainly what none of the techniques can do: "specifications using the techniques cannot be given for programs involving parallel activity." Error handling is treated as unfinished across all five.

## 3. The mechanism or the mathematics, plainly

The paper first shows the wrong way, to make the right ways visible. An input–output specification of push, written over a record with a data array and a top index, says that after the push every old element is unchanged, the new element sits at the new top, and the top index has grown by one. This is correct and it is bad. It describes a particular representation, so the fact that pop returns the most recently pushed value has to be inferred from array indices rather than read. That is the minimality criterion failing in front of the reader.

Then the five techniques, in the paper's order. The first fixes one mathematical discipline for every abstraction. The example is Earley's V-graphs: a data structure is a graph of nodes, atoms and labelled links, and an operation is a transformation of the graph, often drawn as a before-and-after picture. The second lets the specifier choose any established discipline that fits. The example is Hoare's specification of sets in set-theoretic notation, where insert is written as set union. Both of these build a model. The paper's line is exact: "Techniques in the first two categories use the abstract model approach, while those in the remaining categories use the implicit definition approach."

The third technique is the state machine, attributed to Parnas. Operations are split into V-operations, which return a value and do not change state, and O-operations, which change state and return nothing. The specification says how each O-operation changes the results of the V-operations. For the stack, PUSH is an O-operation and its effect is stated through TOP, a V-operation. No state is named; the states are whatever the V-operations can distinguish.

The fourth is axiomatic description in the style of Peano. Legal expressions are built from the operations, and axioms say which expressions denote the same value. For the stack this takes nine axioms: two for domains, one induction axiom restricting stacks to those constructible by the operations, two to keep distinct pushes distinct, and four for TOP and POP.

The fifth is the algebraic method, attributed to Zilles. The domain axioms and the induction axiom are replaced by a functionality declaration, and the distinctness axioms are dropped because the algebraic construction makes non-equivalent expressions distinct by default. What remains is the functionality and four equations. For the stack: TOP(PUSH(S,I)) = I; TOP(CREATE) = INTEGERERROR; POP(PUSH(S,I)) = S; POP(CREATE) = STACKERROR. The stack is these four equations. Nothing else is said, and nothing else is needed.

## 4. What it demonstrated or how it was used, as reported

The paper is a survey and a criterion set, so its demonstration is the comparison itself, carried out on one example. What it shows is that each criterion is paid for by another. The abstract-model techniques score well on constructibility and comprehensibility inside their range, because a reader who knows graphs or sets can picture the thing. They score badly on minimality and range: the V-graph of a set exposes access paths that set membership never needs, and no fixed discipline fits every concept. The implicit-definition techniques have no representational bias, so they reach further and stay minimal. They pay in constructibility and comprehensibility, because the reader must reason from equations to a picture rather than from a picture to equations.

Within the implicit family the paper reports finer differences. The state machine is minimal and free of representation, but it suffers from what the paper calls delayed effects: a change made by one O-operation may only be visible after later operations, and describing this needs hidden functions, which erode minimality. Its extensibility is uneven, since adding a V-operation may require revising every O-operation. The Peano-style axiomatization is minimal, wide and extensible, since one usually adds axioms rather than changing them, but an incomplete axiom set may be satisfied by several different value sets, and the implementer must then show which one is meant. The algebraic method inherits those strengths, is shorter, and by its construction precludes multiple satisfying value sets. Its cost is the same as the axiomatic one: it is hard to write and hard to read, and the paper says it requires a developed methodology it did not yet have.

The paper does not report a large program specified by any method. It reports the stack. It says that combinations will likely be needed for large programs, since different abstractions suit different methods. It reports three open problems as open: parallel programs cannot be specified by any of the five; system-level specifications remain descriptive rather than formal; and error handling is inadequate throughout. The acknowledgement thanks Jack Dennis and the referees. As a piece of use, the paper was the survey that later work on abstract data types cited as the frame, and the memo version circulated from MIT's Computation Structures Group. Phient reports that as a fact about the record, since the citation trail is in the sources of section 16, and does not add a count it has not verified.

## 5. The one idea restated

A thing is what its operations do to each other, and a description of the thing is good exactly to the degree that it says that and nothing else.

That is the whole paper in one sentence, and each half carries one of the paper's two parts. The first half is the definition of a data abstraction: behaviour observed only through the operations. It licenses the implicit-definition approach, because if nothing can be observed except through operations, then equations between operations are a complete description. The second half is the criterion of minimality, with the other five criteria arranged around it. Formality makes the description checkable. Constructibility and comprehensibility are the costs the writer and reader pay. Range and extensibility are what the description is worth over time.

The stack makes the sentence concrete. TOP(PUSH(S,I)) = I is a fact about two operations under composition. It says nothing about arrays, indices, or memory. A reader who knows only those four equations knows every observable fact about stacks. An implementer who satisfies those four equations has built a stack, whatever the representation. The equations are the interface; the representation is private.

The sentence also explains why the paper refuses to pick a winner. Each technique is a way of saying what the operations do to each other, and each pays for its clarity in one currency or another. A model is easy to read and leaks its representation. Equations leak nothing and are hard to read. The criteria are the exchange rate, and the paper's conclusion is that a large program will pay in several currencies at once. That refusal is itself part of the idea: the criteria are fixed, the technique is a choice.

Phient restates it once more in its own words. A word is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions. That is the definition half. Phical stores no point. That is the minimality half. The paper of 1975 is the first place in Liskov's record where those two halves are set side by side as criteria rather than as a language design, and that is why this paper reads it as a claim about language before it reads it as a claim about programs. The rest of this paper asks what the equations of a word would be, and what Phient has measured of them so far.

## 6. The word as a function

In phinet a word is a function from a channel to a phasor: 32 to 64 relations, each a complex number on the closed circle with a count. Nothing about that function is observable except by applying it. Ask the word on channel k and you get a phase; compose that with another word's phase on the same channel and you get a cosine; rank a pool by that cosine and you get a reading. There is no operation that returns the word.

That is Liskov and Zilles' definition of a data abstraction turned toward language. The class of objects is the lexicon. The operations are the readings: `on(about, ·, k)` in the quest, the hinge's two cosines in training, `hexagram_of` in the helix, the nearest-k in decoding. The constraint that behaviour can be observed only by applications of the operations is not a rule Phient chose. It is what a phasor on a circle forces. A phase has no meaning alone. A pair of phases under a channel has one.

The paper's six criteria then become six questions about the word function. Formality: is the reading a mathematical operation a machine can check? It is; phinet matches the PyTorch reference on 62/62 parity tests, and `phinet::sphere` matches scipy's spherical harmonics to a maximum difference of 2.4e-15. Constructibility: can a facet be built without undue difficulty and be sure of capturing the concept? The trainer builds it from a corpus in seconds, and whether it captures the concept is what the MRR measures. Comprehensibility: can a trained reader reconstruct the concept from the function? This is the helix's job, which prints each word as 64 codons with a colour, and it is the criterion Phient has the least evidence for. Minimality: does the function say more than it needs to? Section 10 takes this up, because the byte channel says less than it should, which is the criterion's other failure. Range: does the same function shape describe Austen, Chesterton, Carroll and a synthetic oracle? It does, with the numbers in section 11. Extensibility: does a small change to the lexicon produce a small change to the function? A new relation is one more channel coupled; nothing else moves.

So the word as a function passes the paper's definition and can be interrogated by the paper's criteria. What it does not yet have is the paper's second part: an explicit statement, in equations, of what the readings must satisfy. That is the gap section 8 and section 12 address.

## 7. The relation channel as a coordinate

The state machine technique in the paper draws a line through the operations. V-operations observe. O-operations change. A specification says how each O-operation alters what the V-operations will report. No state is named; the state is whatever the V-operations can tell apart. The paper's complaint is delayed effects: a change may be invisible until later operations expose it, and describing that requires hidden functions.

Phinet has the same line, and the channel is where it is drawn. Coupling is the O-operation: `Trainer::couple_relations` moves a pair on one kind's channel alone, the head's source side toward the other's target side, by a nudge of 0.06·sin d. Reading is the V-operation: the cosine on that channel, ranked over a pool. The specification of a facet, if one were written in Parnas's form, would say that after coupling (about, kind, other), the reading `on(about, ·, kind)` ranks `other` higher than before, and that readings on every other channel are unchanged. The channel is the coordinate that makes the second clause sayable. Without it every O-operation would touch every V-operation, and the specification would be as long as the training log.

The quest of docs/83 measured exactly this clause. On the synthetic oracle, the reading on the kind's own channel reached 0.594 MRR with attraction-only training and dithered rounding, hit@1 34.8%, hit@5 97.4%, within-kind 0.747. The reading on the wrong channel was 0.015 and the offset-channel reading 0.005, both at or under chance MRR 0.021. That is the state machine's separation, measured: an O-operation on channel k moved the V-operation on channel k and left the V-operations on other channels where they were.

Delayed effects appear in phinet too, and in the paper's sense. A coupling that lands under half a quantum does nothing visible now and nothing visible later; nearest rounding froze every pair 8 quanta short. That is an effect delayed forever, and the hidden function needed to describe it is the byte. Dither is the repair, and it is a repair at the level of the O-operation, not the specification. The paper's warning holds: a specification that needs a hidden function to describe the representation's delays has lost minimality, and Phient's facet, specified honestly, would need the byte named. The channel as coordinate makes the honest specification short. The byte makes it one line longer than it should be.

## 8. The operator as X ⇒ Y under c

The algebraic method writes an abstraction as equations between compositions of operations. TOP(PUSH(S,I)) = I. POP(PUSH(S,I)) = S. Each equation is a claim that one composition, applied, yields the same value as another, and the condition is the composition itself. Read as Phical writes it: PUSH followed by TOP, under the condition that the stack was S and the item I, gives I. X ⇒ Y under c, with X the stack state, Y the observed item, and c the operation applied.

The three operators of docs/86 are already in this form, and the paper lets Phient say so exactly. `better(pred, a, b)` is cos(pred, a) − cos(pred, b). It is a comparison between two applications of the same reading under one condition, the prediction. `worse(pred, a, after, continuations)` composes: apply a, then read the best continuation from the new context, and subtract. That is TOP(PUSH(S,I)) written for words: push a onto the context, read the top, compare with what was read before the push. `loss` is the hinge over `better`, the equation with a margin: better ≥ 0.1, or pay the difference.

An algebraic specification of the word operator would be a small set of such equations. Here is one candidate, offered as a candidate and not as a measured property. Antisymmetry: better(p, a, b) = −better(p, b, a). Composition: for a context S and a word a, worse(p, a, after, C) ≤ 0 when a has a continuation at least as strong as its own fit, and > 0 when it does not. Channel locality: on(x, y, k) after coupling (x, k′, z) with k′ ≠ k equals on(x, y, k) before. The unit tests of `phinet::liskov` check the first and a version of the second: antisymmetry, that a dead-end move is refuted, and that forcing prefers the move with a future. The third is what the wrong-channel reading of 0.015 measured on the quest. None of these are yet written as the facet's specification.

The paper's argument for the algebraic form over the Peano form is that the construction itself makes distinct expressions distinct, so the distinctness axioms are dropped. Phinet's circle does the same by geometry: two words with different phases on a channel are different by construction, and the specification does not need to say so. What the circle does not do by construction is keep them different after rounding. The byte can collapse two expressions that the equations hold apart. Section 10 returns to that. The operator is X ⇒ Y under c; the paper's method says to write that down as equations and prove the implementation against them.

## 9. Her idea as a claim about language

Liskov and Zilles wrote about programs. Read as a claim about language, the paper says three things, and each lands in a Phient component.

First: meaning is observable only through operations. There is no way to look at a stack except by pushing and popping. There is no way to look at a word except by relating it. This lands in phinet as the reading, and in phiano as the Facet, which stores relations and answers questions with them. The persona work is the test of whether the operations alone carry enough. Three writers, held-out four-sentence paragraphs, chance 33%: attribution 71% overall, Chesterton 74, Austen 65, Carroll 86. Dialogue replies judged as their persona 44% by the sector reader and 72% by a Kneser-Ney panel. Those are readings of readings, and no operation in the chain ever returned a word's value.

Second: the right unit of specification is the abstraction, not the statement. For language this says that the unit of meaning is the word with its relations, and not the token, not the sentence. Phiecs holds this: a quest is the paradigm asked of an oracle one word at a time, and the table it writes has one row per relation, `about kind other weight`. The kind word standing between the pair is what `RelationVocab::learn` reads as a marker, and `is-a` becomes a channel with no mapping written anywhere. That is the paper's multi-procedure module: the operations of a word are grouped by the word and share its representation.

Third: a specification is also an axiom for other proofs. Once a word's relations are fixed, other words may be read against them. The helix is this in a filed form: 18,496 words as DNA-coded trees, 64 codons each, 1.9 seconds, 75 MB, byte-exact round trip. Each entry is a specification other readings can use, and the round trip is the check that the specification and the implementation agree.

What the claim about language does not yet say is which equations the word must satisfy. Liskov and Zilles gave the stack four. Phient has three operators and one channel-locality result, and no document that states them as the facet's contract. The paper's argument is that until such a statement exists, correctness cannot be proved, only measured. Phient measures. That is the honest position, and it is the position the paper calls provisional.

## 10. Minimality, the byte, and the three operators

Minimality is the criterion the paper presses hardest. A specification must say what is interesting and not what is incidental, and the incidental thing is nearly always the representation. The input–output specification of push fails because it names the array. The V-graph of a set fails because it names access paths. The paper's remedy is to describe operations only in terms of each other.

Phient's representation is a byte per channel: 256 quanta of 2π, 0.0245 rad each. Docs/83 found that the byte leaks into the behaviour. A nudge of 0.06·sin d falls under half a quantum once sin d < 0.2, at 11°, and nearest rounding then writes back the byte it read. Every pair froze 8 quanta short. The reading was correct and the implementation satisfied no equation that a reasonable specification would state, since POP(PUSH(S,I)) = S has an analogue here, that coupling toward a target reaches the target, and it did not. The paper's language for this is exact: an implementation detail became observable through the operations, and the specification, to be truthful, would have to name it. That is the minimality failure in its other direction. The representation did not say too much; it did too little, and what it did was visible.

Dither is the repair that keeps the specification minimal. With rounding up by the fractional quantum, the expected movement equals the nudge, and the equation holds in expectation. The cost is measured: corpus attribution 71% → 65%, so dither ships off by default. That is a trade of one criterion against another, of the paper's kind. The equation is truer with dither. The reading is worse.

The three operators are the other half of the answer. Better, worse and loss are defined only through readings: two cosines, a composition, a margin. They name no byte. When the quest was scored by them and filed by hexagram, 256 words occupied 61 of 64 hexagrams, mean better −0.008, mean worse −0.008, mean loss 0.108 at a margin of 0.1. The table was flat, and docs/86 says why: on this quest the truth ties the best wrong word on almost every row, which is the byte ceiling seen through the third operator. The operators are minimal in the paper's sense, and precisely because they are, they show the representation's failure without describing it. That is what the paper says a good specification does. It does not mention the array. It makes the array's mistake visible at the interface.

## 12. The experiment that would test her idea inside phinet

The paper's claim, made testable, is this: an abstraction specified by equations between its operations can have its implementation checked against those equations, and the check will find representation leaks that readings alone do not.

The experiment writes the facet's algebraic specification and runs it as a test. Four equations, in the paper's stack form. Identity: on(x, y, k) before any coupling equals the untrained reading. Effect: after couple(x, k, y), on(x, y, k) exceeds its previous value by at least one quantum's worth of cosine. Locality: after couple(x, k′, y) with k′ ≠ k, on(x, ·, k) is unchanged over the whole pool. Reversal: after couple(x, k, y) followed by the opposite nudge, on(x, y, k) returns to within one quantum of its start. The fourth is POP(PUSH(S,I)) = S for words, and it is the one the byte will break.

The arm is `bin/quest_ingest` on the synthetic oracle of 256 words, 1,280 rows, with the four equations checked after every coupling, with dithered rounding and attraction-only training, which is the shipped-best configuration at 0.594 MRR. The control is the same run with nearest rounding, the shipped default that reached 0.082 MRR. The paper predicts that the readings will separate the arms, and that is already known. What the experiment adds is which equation fails first, and where. The prediction from docs/83 is that Effect fails under nearest rounding once sin d < 0.2, and that Reversal fails under both, since dither restores movement in expectation and not per step.

A second arm tests the algebraic method's own claim, that distinct expressions are distinct by construction. Take pairs of words the oracle holds apart under one kind, and count, after training, how many share a byte on that channel. The measure is the number of collapsed pairs per channel. The control is the untrained facet, where collapse is whatever the initialisation gives. If the byte collapses pairs that the equations distinguish, the specification is minimal and the representation is not, in the paper's sense.

The outputs are three: a pass/fail per equation per arm, a count of collapsed pairs per channel, and the existing MRR, so that the equations can be set beside the reading they are meant to explain. The cost is small; the quest runs in seconds. What the experiment does not do is test comprehensibility. That would need a reader, and no reader is in the loop. It is proposed here and not run, and nothing in section 11 comes from it.

## 13. Where we disagree or the analogy breaks

The paper's abstraction is exact. A stack either satisfies POP(PUSH(S,I)) = S or it does not. Phient's word satisfies its equations to within a cosine, and the cosine is measured over a pool, and the pool changes. The equations of section 8 are inequalities with margins, and a margin is a threshold someone chose. The paper's minimality criterion would call the margin an implementation detail that has leaked into the specification. Phient has no way to remove it. Meaning as a side effect of pairs moving is not an algebra with equalities. It is an algebra with approximations, and the paper does not treat those.

The paper's operations are total or fail with a named error. TOP(CREATE) = INTEGERERROR. Phient's readings never fail; they return a rank, and a rank of last is still a rank. The paper says error handling is inadequate across all five techniques, so this is a gap on both sides, but it is a wider gap in Phient, where there is no error value at all. A word with no relation on a channel reads as a phase anyway, the phase it was initialised with.

The paper's abstraction is sequential, and the paper says so: none of the techniques can specify parallel activity. Phinet trains with rayon and couples many pairs per epoch, and the order matters, since each coupling reads a byte another coupling may have just written. A specification in the paper's form would have to fix an order, or state equations that hold for every order, and neither has been done. This is the paper's own open problem and Phient inherits it unsolved.

Where the analogy is strongest it is also weakest. The paper's argument for the implicit approach is that it removes representational bias. Phient's representation is the meaning. A phasor on a circle with a count is what the paradigm says a word is. To specify it implicitly, by its readings alone, is to omit the paradigm from the specification. Liskov and Zilles would accept that, since the specification is meant to be independent of the representation. Phient cannot accept it fully, because the circle, the sphere and the hexagram are not interchangeable implementations of one concept; the anisotropic tie reads differently from the circle, 16.0× against 14.7×, and the difference is the point. The paper's separation of interface from representation is a discipline Phient uses. It is not a description of what Phient is.

## 14. The person and the moment

The facts here are those the sources of section 16 verify. Barbara Liskov was born Barbara Jane Huberman in 1939. She took her PhD at Stanford in 1968 under John McCarthy with a thesis titled "A Program to Play Chess End Games." She worked at MITRE, and after Stanford returned there as research staff. By 1975 she was at MIT's Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science, which is the affiliation on the paper. Her co-author, Stephen N. Zilles, was at IBM Research in San Jose, and the memo version of the paper notes support from IBM funds for computer science research. The acknowledgement thanks Jack Dennis and the referees.

The year before, in 1974, Liskov and Zilles had published "Programming with abstract data types" in ACM SIGPLAN Notices, volume 9, number 4, pages 50 to 59. That paper proposed the abstraction; this one asks how to specify it. The two are a pair, and the second is the more cautious. It commits to no language and no technique. It commits to six criteria and a definition, and it surveys the field as it stood, including Zilles's own algebraic method, without declaring a winner.

The paper appeared twice. It was presented at the International Conference on Reliable Software and printed in its proceedings, which ACM distributed as SIGPLAN Notices volume 10, number 6, June 1975. It was also printed in the first issue of the IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, volume SE-1, number 1, March 1975, pages 7 to 19. The MIT Computation Structures Group circulated it as a memo, and MIT's CSAIL archive still serves that memo. The paper stood in the first issue of the journal that would define the field. Software engineering as a discipline with a transactions journal, and data abstraction as one of its founding questions, arrive in the same number.

The ACM Turing Award followed in 2008, with a citation for contributions to "programming language and system design, especially related to data abstraction, fault tolerance, and distributed computing." This paper is the second of those three named, in its earliest form, and the first of the three is CLU, which was being built at MIT as the paper was written. Phient does not state when she joined MIT or who else was on the CLU group, since the sources reached in this session do not settle those details and the brief says not to guess.

## 15. What remains

The facet has no written specification. Section 8 proposes four equations and section 12 proposes to run them. Until they are written into a test and run against both rounding modes, Phient's claim that the operators are minimal in the paper's sense is an argument and not a measurement. This is the first thing that remains, and it is small.

Comprehensibility has no measurement at all. The helix prints a word as 64 codons with wavelengths and hexagrams, and round-trips byte-exact, but no one has been asked to reconstruct a word's concept from its codons and say whether they could. The paper is clear that this is a criterion in its own right, and Phient has treated it as a display. A reader study is the honest next step, and it is not planned.

The three operators are not yet the names the training loop exposes. Today `loss` is the tape's hinge and `better` is implicit in it; docs/86 says so. Making the three names the interface would be the paper's multi-procedure module for the trainer: the operations grouped, the representation behind them. That is a refactor with a specification attached, and the specification comes first.

Forcing at depth two on the quest has not been run. On text, one-step lookahead did not beat greedy at any λ tried, and docs/86 says why: every word has some continuation. The quest is where `worse` should bite, since a word either has onward relations or does not, and that run remains to be made. The Phi-4 quest remains too, and every quest number in this paper is the synthetic oracle.

The paper's own three open problems remain open in Phient. Parallel coupling has no specification. System-level description, meaning what phinet, phiano and phiecs promise each other, is prose in docs/85 and not equations. Error handling does not exist as a value; a missing relation reads as an initial phase. Of these the second is nearest. Docs/85 already draws the boundary rule, that a child of phinet is anything that reads the 64 channels its own way and needs no words, and that rule is the beginning of a specification for the family.

Last, the held-out quest numbers. One hop at 0.011, two hops is-a 0.095. A specification written and checked will not change these. They are about generalisation, which the paper does not address, and Phient should not expect a proof discipline to supply what a measurement has shown missing.

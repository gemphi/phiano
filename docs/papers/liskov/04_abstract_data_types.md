# A type is its operations: the cluster read as a word

*Phient interpreted papers · Liskov series, 4 of 16 · 2026-02-23 · on: "Programming with Abstract Data Types" (Barbara Liskov and Stephen Zilles, 1974, ACM SIGPLAN Symposium on Very High Level Languages, SIGPLAN Notices 9(4):50–59)*

In Phient's world, a word (w) is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions (c). The Phical paradigm, X ⇒ Y, drives and composes Phient's Phasor Language Models: Phinet, Phiano, PhiTon. Phical stores no point: a word is 32–64 relations, each a complex phasor on a closed circle with a count; meaning is a side effect of pairs of words moving under a condition.

## 1. Our abstract

This is the fourth of sixteen papers in which Phient reads one work by Barbara Liskov through its own paradigm. The work is the short 1974 paper with Stephen Zilles that gave the field the phrase "abstract data type" and the construct that became CLU's cluster. It is ten pages. It has no experiment, no measurement and no proof. Its claim is a definition and a language sketch: a data type is a class of objects characterized completely by the operations available on them, and a language should let a programmer write such a type as a program, an operation cluster, whose representation is hidden from every other program.

We read it here because the definition is the paradigm sentence at the top of this page in another vocabulary. Liskov and Zilles say a type is nothing but its operations. Phient says a word is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions. In both, the object has no inside that matters. A stack is what push, pop and top do to it. A word is what 32 to 64 channels say it does to other words. The representation exists, and in Phient it is a byte per channel, but it is the cluster's business and no one else's.

Sections 2 to 5 state what the paper says, in its terms, from the sources we could reach. We could not read the paper's full text at the ACM Digital Library; section 16 lists what we did read, including Liskov's own 1992 history of CLU, which quotes and dates the work. Sections 6 to 10 place the cluster inside the Phient family: the word as an abstract type, the relation channel as the hidden rep, the operator X ⇒ Y as a cluster operation invoked under a condition, and the paper's separation of specification from implementation as the split between the quest's oracle and the trained function. Section 11 lists what Phient has measured that bears on any of this, all of it from the facts file. Section 12 names an experiment with its control. Section 13 says where the analogy breaks. Section 14 is about the person and the year. Section 15 is what is left.

One limit up front. Phient has no type system, no compiler and no static check. Nothing in gemphi enforces an abstraction boundary the way the paper wants a language to. That is a weaker thing than a cluster. The paper is read here as a claim about what a type is, and that claim Phient has adopted whole.

## 2. What the work claims, in its terms

The paper opens from the motive of the symposium it was given at. Its abstract begins: "The motivation behind the work in very-high-level languages is to ease the programming task by providing the programmer with a language containing primitives or abstractions suitable to his problem area." The difficulty, as the abstract goes on, is that a language designer cannot know in advance which abstractions a programmer will need. The paper's answer is to let the programmer add them. The language should, in the paper's words, "provide a mechanism whereby the language can be extended to contain the abstractions which the user requires."

The frame is structured programming. The paper says that "structured programming attempts to impose a discipline on the programming task so that the resulting programs are 'well-structured'", and that a problem is solved by successive decomposition into a program that runs on an abstract machine providing just the objects and operations the problem needs. Liskov's history of CLU quotes the joint paper's own statement of this: the programmer writes "a program which solves the problem but which runs on an abstract machine, i.e., one which provides just those data objects and operations which are suitable to solving the problem." Procedures had long served as the abstractions of control. The paper's claim is that data needs the same treatment, and that the two existing tools, built-in types and procedural abstraction, do not give it.

The definition is the paper's center: "An abstract data type defines a class of abstract objects which is completely characterized by the operations available on those objects. This means that an abstract data type can be defined by defining the characterizing operations for that type." What makes an abstraction useful, the paper says, is that it "allows us to express all relevant details for use of the abstraction, but hides all irrelevant details." The representation is an irrelevant detail to every program except the one that implements the type.

From this the paper draws its language requirement. A structured language, in its words, "contains no preconceived notions about the particular set of useful abstractions", and it must be strongly typed. Liskov's history quotes the requirement as it stood in 1973: the language must be "strongly typed so that it is not possible to make use of an object in any other way except by calling its operations." That sentence is the whole claim. The type is the operations; the language exists to make that true rather than merely advisory.

## 3. The mechanism, plainly

The mechanism is a program. The paper says that "an abstract data type is realized by writing a special kind of program, called an operation cluster", and the cluster has three parts. There is a description of the representation, a piece of code that creates objects, and the definitions of the operations. Lawrence Flon's survey of the same year, written at Carnegie Mellon with the paper in hand, describes the proposal the same way: a cluster "consists of several parts, including a representation, operations, and a list of those operations whose names are to be known outside the scope of the cluster definition." The list of exported names is the interface. Everything not on it is invisible.

The representation is introduced by a reserved word. The paper says "the rep description defines a new type, denoted by the reserved word rep", and that this type is accessible only within the cluster and describes how objects are viewed there. Outside the cluster an object of the abstract type is opaque. Inside, the operations see it as the rep and manipulate it as such. The example the paper works through is a stack, written as a parameterized cluster, stack: cluster(element_type: type), with operations that the morning-paper summary lists as push, pop, top, erasetop and empty. The rep of the stack is a vector of elements and an index to the top, which is to say a plain data structure that the world never sees.

Invocation carries the type name. An operation is called as type_name$operation(args). The paper says "there are several reasons why the type name is included in the operation call": the call names both the abstraction and the operation, so the compiler can find the cluster and check the types, and two clusters may both have an operation called push without collision. Declarations of abstract types use the same syntax as declarations of built-in types. A user-defined stack should look to the program like an integer does.

Type checking is static. Liskov's history says that the 1973 workshop discussions led the group to realize that "complete static type checking is possible", where J. H. Morris's 1973 proposal had enforced the boundary at run time with keys. The paper extends the checking to type parameters. Finally the paper separates two structures of a program: "we believe it is helpful to associate two structures with a program: its logical structure and its physical structure." The cluster is a unit of the logical structure. The compiler is charged with mapping it to an efficient physical one, because "a good logical structure does not necessarily imply a good physical structure."

## 4. What it demonstrated, as reported

The paper demonstrated nothing in the measured sense. It reported a design. What it showed is that the definition could be written down as language and that a familiar type could be expressed in it. Liskov's own account of the reception is the most useful thing we have. She writes that her work on the idea ran from "the latter part of 1972 through the summer of 1973", that she had grown "dissatisfied with the papers on programming methodology" because their notion of a module remained "nebulous", and that the step forward came when she "noticed that many of the modules discussed in the papers on modularity were defining data types." A module with an encapsulated representation and a set of operations was a type. That observation is what the 1974 paper reports.

The collaboration is dated. At the Savannah workshop in April 1973 Liskov "began to work with Steve Zilles, who was also at M.I.T. working on a similar idea", and they refined the concept over that spring and summer with Austin Henderson as "an interested listener and critic." A first version appeared in September 1973; the version read here was given in April 1974 at the Very High Level Languages symposium and printed in SIGPLAN Notices 9(4), pages 50 to 59. The term moved during that year. The 1973 draft spoke of a "function cluster"; by the later version "an abstract data type is implemented by a 'cluster' containing a description of the representation and implementations of all the operations." The language that grew from the paper took its name from the construct: "CLU was chosen as the name of the language in the fall of 1973. The name was selected because it is the first three letters of 'cluster'."

Zilles's part was the specification side. At a Harvard meeting in October 1973 he "described his early work on specifying abstract data types; he gave the axioms for the soon to be notorious stack example." The paper itself does not do algebraic specification, so far as the sources we reached report, but the stack it implements as a cluster is the stack Zilles was axiomatizing. The 1974 NSF proposal drew the line the paper had implied: "An abstract data type is a concept whose meaning is captured in a set of specifications, while a cluster provides an implementation of a data type." Flon's survey, five months after the symposium, was already comparing the cluster with Morris's proposal. The wider use of the idea is the history of CLU and of the 1977 CACM paper on abstraction mechanisms in CLU. That much the sources report; the paper reports only the proposal.

## 5. The one idea restated

A type is what you can do with it. Nothing else about it is part of its meaning. The paper says this in a single sentence and then spends nine pages making a language keep the promise. The promise has two halves, and both matter. The first half is that the operations characterize the type completely: if you know what push, pop and top do to each other, you know what a stack is, and there is no further fact to learn. The second half is that the operations are the only access: no program other than the cluster can see the vector and the index, so no program can depend on them, so the cluster can change them and nothing outside breaks.

The first half is a claim about meaning. The second is a claim about language. The paper's contribution is to insist they are the same claim. A definition by operations that a language does not enforce is a convention, and conventions fail at scale. A language that enforces boundaries without a definition of what the boundary contains is a protection mechanism with nothing to protect. The abstract data type is the point where the two meet: the meaning of the type is the set of operations, and the language makes that set the only door.

Restated for Phient, the sentence becomes the paradigm sentence. A word is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions. The relations are the operations. The condition is the type name in front of the dollar sign. A word has no inside that the rest of the system may consult. What it has is 32 to 64 channels, each a phasor with a count, and those are the rep. The operator that moves one word toward another under a condition is the cluster's operation, and it is the only way Phient uses a word. Where the paper wants a compiler to reject every other access, Phient has only the fact that no other access has been written. Section 13 returns to how much weaker that is.

One more thing the sentence carries. If the type is its operations, then two clusters with the same operations, behaving the same way, are the same type, whatever their reps. The paper does not say this in 1974; the later work on subtyping does. But it is already latent in the definition, and Phient's helix already relies on it: the same word can be filed by channel, by hexagram, by sphere degree or by codon, and it is the same word because the operations that read it agree.

## 6. The word as an abstract type

Take the paper's definition and substitute. An abstract word defines a class of abstract objects which is completely characterized by the relations available on those objects. This is what Phical means when it says it stores no point. A vector embedding is a rep offered as a meaning: here are 300 numbers, and the meaning of the word is the position they name. The paper would call that a confusion of physical with logical structure. Phient's word is not a position. It is a table of 32 to 64 relations, each a phasor on a closed circle with a count, and the table is read only through operations that compare it with another word's table under a condition.

The cluster's three parts map cleanly. The rep is the byte per channel: 256 quanta of 2π, with a count alongside. The creation code is the Facet and the Trainer in phiano, which build a word's table from a corpus or from the quest's rows. The operations are the operator X ⇒ Y and, since docs/86, the three named comparisons better, worse and loss. Everything that a phiano persona, a quest score or a helix codon does with a word goes through one of those.

The paper's stack is parameterized on its element type. Phient's word is parameterized in the same way on its condition. A stack of integers and a stack of strings share one cluster and differ in one parameter. A word under is-a and the same word under part-of share one table and differ in which channel the condition selects. The quest's five kinds, is-a, part-of, near, used-for and unlike, are the element types of the word cluster. Wrong channel reads 0.015 MRR against 0.594 within the right one, which is what a type parameter is for: the operation on the wrong instantiation is meaningless, and the paper's compiler would have rejected it before it ran.

What the substitution does not carry is the second half of the promise. The paper's cluster hides the rep because the language refuses every other access. Phient's byte hides the rep because there is little to see: a phase in 256 quanta is opaque by poverty, not by rule. The deadband, where nudges below half a quantum vanish and nearest rounding froze every pair 8 quanta short, is the rep leaking through the abstraction. The paper's answer to a rep too coarse for its operations is to change the rep without touching the callers. That is the answer Phient gave, dithered rounding, and it was possible only because the callers never saw the byte.

## 7. The relation channel as the hidden rep

The paper says the rep "defines a new type, denoted by the reserved word rep", visible only inside the cluster. The rep is not nothing. It is a full type of its own, with its own operations, and the cluster's code manipulates it freely. What the paper forbids is that anything outside the cluster do so. Phient's channel is this kind of thing. A channel is a coordinate on the relation circle, one of 32 or 64, and inside the engine it is a complex phasor that the unitary FFT rotates, the Tape differentiates and the sphere tie reweights by spherical-harmonic degree. Outside the engine it has no name a persona or a quest can call.

This is the reading Phient gives to the paper's rep: a coordinate is a representation detail. That the word's 64 channels are Y_l^m with l < 8 on a sphere, or 64 sectors on a circle, or 64 codons in base-4 ACGT, is a choice of rep. The helix of docs/84 is the demonstration. The same 18,496 words are written as a DNA-coded tree, 64 codons, with B-DNA geometry of 10.5 codons per turn and 0.34 nm rise, and read back byte-exact in 1.9 s and 75 MB. Nothing that uses the words changes. The rep changed and the operations did not, which is the paper's test for a correct abstraction.

The sphere tie makes the same point with a number. Tie::Sphere gives one complex weight per spherical-harmonic degree, 1,192 params, and on Austen hinge at 3 epochs scores 12.8× valid against the circle's 14.7× at 8,360 params. Tie::Anisotropic, one weight per (l, |m|), 36 slots, 4,776 params, scores 16.0× valid and beats the circle. Three reps for one type, three physical structures for one logical one. The paper's sentence applies verbatim: "a good logical structure does not necessarily imply a good physical structure", and the job of choosing the physical one belongs inside the cluster. The trainer chooses the tie; the persona that reads the word does not know which tie was chosen.

Where the paper and Phient part on the rep is in who declares it. The paper's rep is whatever the cluster writer declares. Phient's rep is fixed by the paradigm at 32 to 64 channels, each a byte and a count. This is a decision made at the level of the language rather than the cluster, and the paper would call it the "preconceived notion" a structured language should not contain. Phient's defense is that the word is the only type it has. Whether one cluster is enough is the question the whole family is asking, and section 13 says why the paper's answer would be no.

## 8. The operator as type$operation under c

The paper writes an operation call as type_name$operation(args), and gives reasons for putting the type name in front: the compiler can find the cluster, the types can be checked, and two clusters can share an operation name. Phient's operator is written X ⇒ Y under c, and the condition c plays the part of the type name. The same word, pushed toward another word, is a different operation under is-a than under near. The condition selects the channel, and the channel is the cluster the operation belongs to. When docs/83 reports within-kind MRR 0.747 and wrong-channel 0.015, it is reporting that the operation invoked under the wrong type name does nothing useful, which in the paper's language means the call would not have compiled.

The three operators of docs/86 are the clearer case, because they are named. better(pred, a, b) is the difference of two cosines. worse(pred, a, after, continuations) is the gap between a word's own fit and the best continuation after it. loss(pred, target, decoy, margin) is the hinge over better. These are the operations of the word cluster in the paper's sense: they are the only three ways the training loop and the decoder are supposed to interrogate a word, and each takes words and returns a comparison, never a channel. The forcing_choice that picks the largest cos − λ·worse is a program written against the interface, and it does not know or need to know the rep.

The paper's other reason for the type name, that the reader sees at the call what abstraction is in play, is a reason Phient has been slow to honor. In the code the hinge is Tape::hinge and better is implicit in it; docs/86 says as much in its list of what remains. By the paper's standard the word cluster is half built. Loss is named. Better is inside it. Worse is new and lives beside the decoder. The paper's discipline, applied, would move all three behind one boundary and make every caller say which one it means.

There is a subtler correspondence in the strong-typing requirement, "not possible to make use of an object in any other way except by calling its operations." An operator X ⇒ Y under c is a use of X and of Y at once, and the paper's check would require both to be words and c a condition the cluster recognizes. Nothing stops a caller passing a condition the word has no count under, and the answer comes back as a phase anyway. The paper would want that to be a compile error. Phient returns a number, and the number is noise.

## 9. Specification and cluster: the oracle and the trained function

The 1974 NSF proposal that followed the paper drew the line that Phient finds most useful: "An abstract data type is a concept whose meaning is captured in a set of specifications, while a cluster provides an implementation of a data type." The type is what the operations are required to do. The cluster is one program that does it. Zilles's axioms for the stack were a specification; the parameterized cluster in the paper was an implementation; a second cluster with another rep would implement the same type.

The quest in phiecs is a specification in this sense. The synthetic oracle gives 256 words in families of 8 under five kinds, 1,280 rows, and it says which pairs of words stand in which relation. It says nothing about channels, phases or counts. It is the axioms of the word type: what the operations must return when asked. The trained function is the cluster. Its rep is the byte table. Its correctness is measured as how often the cluster's operations return what the specification says: 0.594 MRR with attraction-only training and dithered rounding, hit@1 34.8%, hit@5 97.4%, against chance 0.021. The shipped trainer's 0.082 was a cluster that failed its specification. The rep was the same. The operations were the same. The creation code was wrong.

The separation pays off here in a way it did not in 1974, because the specification is now data and the cluster is learned. In the paper the compiler checks types but not behaviour. In Phient the oracle is written, the cluster is trained, and the check is the held-out row. The held-out 20% is a specification the cluster never saw: one hop 0.011, two hops is-a 0.095 and used-for 0.093, the other kinds at chance. That is the honest report on how much of the type's meaning the cluster captured beyond the rows it was built on, and it is little. The cluster implements the training rows and not the type.

The hexagram filing of docs/86 is the paper's export list. Each word is filed under the hexagram its 64-channel function is, and 256 words occupy 61 of 64 hexagrams. The paper's cluster exports the names of the operations the world may call. The hexagram is the name the world may call a word by without seeing its rep. That the mean better and worse are both −0.008 and the mean loss 0.108 at margin 0.1, flat, says the specification is too easy for the export list to sort it: on this quest the truth ties the best wrong word on almost every row. The cluster is fine. The specification does not yet distinguish its objects.

## 10. Where the idea lands: the boundary Phient does not enforce

The paper's mechanism is a boundary, and the question for Phient is where its boundary is and who keeps it. In phinet the answer is the Tensor [b, w, s] complex and the Tape. Those are the rep, in the paper's sense, and the operations the rest of the family calls are the FFT, the ties, the gates and the three operators. In phiano the answer is the Facet: a persona or a decoder asks a Facet for a word's relations under a condition and gets a comparison back. In phiecs the answer is the entity-component split, which is the cluster idea arrived at from the game-engine side: a component is a rep, a system is an operation, and an entity is an object that is nothing but the components attached to it.

The place the boundary holds best is the helix. A codon names a channel in base-4 ACGT; a hexagram names it in 64 sectors; a sphere degree and order name it as Y_l^m. Each is a rep for the same logical structure, and the helix reads and writes all of them byte-exact. PhiTon's wavelength, band and hex per codon are a fourth rep, and LightQuantum, SpectralBand and PhitonColor are the rep type in the paper's sense: a type of its own, with operations of its own, used inside the cluster and invisible outside it. When the paper says a rep "defines a new type", PhiTon is what it means.

The place the boundary holds worst is the deadband. A channel is one byte, 256 quanta of 2π. The nudge 0.06·sin d falls under half a quantum once sin d < 0.2, at 11°, and nearest rounding froze every pair 8 quanta short of the target. The cluster's rep was too coarse for its own operations. The paper's discipline says: fix the rep, keep the interface. Phient did that with dithered rounding, and the interface held. But dither costs corpus attribution, 71% to 65%, so it ships off by default. That is a rep decision leaking into a caller's result, which the paper would call a failure of abstraction. It is also the truth about the byte.

The three operators land as the cluster's public operations, and docs/86 is explicit that they are half exposed. The training loop still calls the hinge by its rep-level name. When the paper says the type name goes in the call so the reader knows which abstraction is in play, it is asking for what docs/86 lists under what remains: better, worse and loss as the three names the training loop exposes. That is the paper's mechanism, applied to Phient, in one sentence.

## 11. What Phient has measured that bears on it

Nothing in gemphi is a type system, so nothing measured is a measurement of the paper's mechanism. What has been measured is whether the word behaves like a type defined by its operations: whether changing the rep leaves the operations intact, whether the operations return what a specification says, and whether a caller can tell which rep is underneath. All numbers are from PHIENT_FACTS.md.

Rep substitution. The circle tie at 8,360 params gives Austen hinge 14.7× valid and 14.6× test at 3 epochs, in 142 s. The sphere tie, one complex weight per spherical-harmonic degree, 1,192 params, gives 12.8× valid and 13.1× test, hit@10 0.182, in 89 s. The anisotropic tie, one weight per (l, |m|), 36 slots, 4,776 params, gives 16.0× valid and 15.6× test, hit@10 0.254, in 85 s. On the synthetic degree-filter task, 200 items and 40 epochs, the circle reaches MRR 0.662 against identity 0.646, and the sphere 1.000. The engine's sphere agrees with scipy sph_harm_y to a maximum difference of 2.4e-15. Three reps, one interface, three scores.

Round trip. The helix writes 18,496 words as a DNA-coded tree in 1.9 s and 75 MB and reads them back byte-exact. The rep changes representation without changing the object.

Specification met. On the synthetic quest, chance 0.021 MRR, the shipped trainer scores 0.082 and attraction-only with dithered rounding 0.594, hit@1 34.8%, hit@5 97.4%, within-kind 0.747, wrong channel 0.015, offset reading 0.005. Held out 20%: one hop 0.011, two hops is-a 0.095, used-for 0.093, part-of, near and unlike about chance.

Operations as interface. Operator on Austen, chance MRR 0.0063: identity 7.9×, hinge 14.7×/14.6×, softmax β=8 18.0×/18.8×, wide and deep at 50,016 params 30.7×/30.3× at 8 epochs, hit@10 0.348. The three operators on decoding, k=32: nearest-k KN perplexity 66.9, forcing λ=0.05 69.7, λ=0.25 68.5, λ=1 107.4 with evaluator 0.712, distinct 0.777 and shared 4-grams 22.3%. On the quest by hexagram: 61 of 64 hexagrams occupied, mean better −0.008, worse −0.008, loss 0.108 at margin 0.1.

Rep leaking. Dither costs attribution 71% to 65% and ships off by default. Parity with the PyTorch reference is 62 of 62 tests.

Engine. The engine is allocation and bandwidth bound at these sizes, a 1 MB op taking 2 to 5 ms, which is the physical structure the paper leaves to the compiler and Phient leaves to the allocator.

Not measured: anything about a second word cluster; anything about a caller that reads a channel directly and what it gains; the operations-only table of section 12; any Phi-4 run; the sphere at softmax or wide and deep.

## 12. The experiment, with its control

The paper's test of an abstraction is whether the rep can be changed without a caller noticing. Phient has run that test by accident three times, with the sphere ties and the helix. The experiment proposed here runs it on purpose and asks the paper's other question: whether the operations characterize the type completely, which is to say whether a caller given only the operations can recover everything the type contains.

Setup. Take the Austen operator as trained, wide and deep, 50,016 params, 30.7× valid at 8 epochs. Freeze it. Build a second word table from nothing but calls to the interface: for every word in the pool of 1,228, record only what better, worse and loss return against every other word under each condition, and never read a channel. From those comparisons alone, fit a new 64-channel table by the same trainer. Call this the operations-only table. Evaluate it on the same held-out split, MRR against chance 0.0063 and hit@10.

Claim under test. If the paper is right about words, the operations-only table should score within the run-to-run spread of the original, because the operations are the whole meaning and the rep adds nothing. If it scores well below, the byte table holds information the three operators do not expose, and the word is not yet an abstract type in the paper's sense: it has an inside that matters.

Control. The control is a table fit from the same number of comparisons drawn with the words shuffled, so that each comparison is a real number from a real call but attached to the wrong pair. This controls for the trainer learning the distribution of comparison values rather than their assignment. A second control is the identity operator, 7.9×, which is the score of a table that has learned nothing from the interface.

Second arm, the rep swap. Repeat with the anisotropic sphere tie as the frozen source and the circle tie as the target. If the operations-only table trained from sphere comparisons scores as a circle table, the interface has carried the type across reps, which is what the paper's cluster is for.

What would count. Operations-only within 10% of the source on valid MRR, and above the shuffled control by the same margin the source is above identity. Anything else says the interface is incomplete, and the next step is to ask which comparison is missing. Not run. The cost is one training run per arm at the Austen scale, which the facts file puts near 142 s per hinge epoch set on the circle and 85 s on the anisotropic sphere.

## 13. Where we disagree, or the analogy breaks

The first break is enforcement. The paper's second half is that a definition by operations means nothing unless the language refuses every other access, at compile time. Phient has no such refusal. The byte table is a Rust struct, and any code in gemphi may read a phase and act on it. That no code does is a fact about the codebase today, not a guarantee. By the paper's standard, Phient's word is a convention, and the paper was written because conventions fail. We accept the criticism.

The second break is the number of types. The paper's mechanism exists so that a programmer may add types the designer did not foresee. Phient has one type, the word, with one rep fixed at 32 to 64 channels. The paradigm's answer is that everything else is a word under a condition: a persona, a quest family, a hexagram. Whether one type parameterized by condition can do what many types do is an open claim, and the held-out quest numbers, one hop 0.011, are evidence that today it cannot.

The third break is behaviour. The paper's compiler checks types, and the specification of what the operations do is left to the programmer, or to Zilles's axioms. Phient's specification is the oracle and its check is MRR. This is a better check than a type check, since it tests behaviour, and a worse one, since it tests it statistically and on the rows it has. A cluster in the paper either implements the stack or does not. A trained word implements the quest 59% of the way, and there is no sense in which that is a type.

The fourth is the word "completely". The paper says the operations characterize the type completely. Phient's three operators are three comparisons, and section 12 asks whether they exhaust the byte table. Docs/86 already gives a reason to doubt it: forcing at λ = 1 raises the evaluator's own score to 0.712 while perplexity worsens to 107.4. Two operations on the same rep disagree about which output is better. In the paper that would be a specification with inconsistent axioms. In Phient it is a Tuesday.

Last, the paper is about programs a person writes. Its author later argued that "a language can guide him or her into thinking about the right things", and that "abstraction is the key to good design." Phient's clusters are not written; they are trained. The guidance a language gives a programmer has no counterpart in a trainer, unless the loss is that guidance. Docs/86 suggests it is, by naming loss as the third operator. That is a reading, and the paper does not support or refuse it.

## 14. The person and the moment

Barbara Liskov, born Barbara Huberman, took her PhD at Stanford in 1968 under John McCarthy with the chess-endgame program that the first paper in this series reads. She then worked at MITRE and in 1972 joined the faculty at MIT. The paper read here is the first she published from MIT on the subject that would occupy her for the next decade. Her own account, written in 1992 as "A History of CLU", dates the thinking to "the latter part of 1972 through the summer of 1973" and says she was at first "not thinking about designing a language" but about the concept.

The moment was the modularity debate. Dijkstra's structured programming and Parnas's papers on module decomposition were in the air, and Liskov writes that by the fall of 1972 she had read them and "became dissatisfied with the papers on programming methodology" because the notion of a module remained "nebulous." Her step was to see that the modules those papers described were, most of them, defining data types. The Savannah workshop in April 1973 put her together with Stephen Zilles, then also at MIT, and the two refined the idea through the summer with Austin Henderson as critic. A first paper went out in September 1973. The name CLU was chosen that fall, from the first three letters of "cluster". In October 1973 at Harvard, Zilles gave the axioms for the stack. In April 1974 the paper read here was delivered at the ACM SIGPLAN Symposium on Very High Level Languages and printed in SIGPLAN Notices 9(4), pages 50 to 59.

Two influences the sources name deserve saying. J. H. Morris's 1973 work on protection had shown an abstract data type implemented as a lambda expression with keys enforcing the boundary at run time; Liskov's group concluded that "complete static type checking is possible" and took that road. Simula 67 had classes that "grouped a set of procedures with some variables" and could be instantiated, and the group noted that "Simula classes did not enforce encapsulation." The 1974 paper sits between the two: Simula's class shape with Morris's boundary, checked before the program runs.

The 1974 NSF proposal, the CLU language, the 1977 CACM paper "Abstraction Mechanisms in CLU" and the 1978 reference manual followed. Lawrence Flon's survey of September 1974 at Carnegie Mellon was already comparing the cluster with other proposals. The Turing Award citation of 2008 names the work on data abstraction first. Everything in this section is from Liskov's history and the ACM record; we have not seen a biography beyond those and state nothing further about the year.

## 15. What remains

The paper leaves Phient one instruction and two questions. The instruction is the one docs/86 already lists: make better, worse and loss the only three names by which the training loop, the decoder and the quest touch a word, so that the word cluster exists as the paper means it, with an export list and nothing else public. Today loss is the tape's hinge and better is implicit. The paper would say the cluster is not written until the calls say type$operation, and it would be right.

The first question is section 12's: do the three operations characterize the word completely, or does the byte table carry meaning that no operation exposes? The experiment is a training run per arm at Austen scale and has not been run. Its answer decides whether Phient's word is an abstract type or a data structure with a polite interface.

The second question is the one Phient has been asking of every paper in the family: whether one type is enough. The paper's mechanism exists to let programmers add types. Phient's paradigm forbids it, and offers the condition as the parameter that does the work of many types. The quest held-out numbers say the parameter does not yet generalize: one hop 0.011, two hops 0.095 on is-a and about chance on three kinds of five. A Phi-4 quest, which would be the first specification not written to be flat, has not been run. Until it is, the claim that a word under a condition can do what a cluster of a new type does is a claim and not a result.

Beyond these, the paper's separation of specification from cluster suggests a discipline Phient has not adopted: write the axioms of the word before training it. The quest oracle is a set of rows. Zilles's stack axioms were equations among operations: pop after push returns the element. The word's equivalent would be equations among better, worse and loss under composition of conditions, and docs/86's antisymmetry test is the only one written. A specification of the word in that form, tested on the trained table, would tell us what the type is independent of any rep, and would give the helix's four reps something to be reps of.

The last item is enforcement. Phient could make the byte table private to phinet and expose only the three operators. That is the paper's proposal, applied. Whether to do it before the experiment says the interface is complete is a question of order, and the paper's remark that program quality is most dependent on good design says to do it first.

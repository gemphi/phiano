# Substitution is the test: is-a read as a claim about programs

*Phient interpreted papers · Liskov series, 13 of 16 · 2026-06-29 · on: "Data Abstraction and Hierarchy" (Barbara Liskov, 1987, keynote address, OOPSLA '87, Orlando; printed in Addendum to the Proceedings, ACM SIGPLAN Notices 23(5):17–34, May 1988)*

In Phient's world, a word (w) is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions (c). The Phical paradigm, X ⇒ Y, drives and composes Phient's Phasor Language Models: Phinet, Phiano, PhiTon. Phical stores no point: a word is 32–64 relations, each a complex phasor on a closed circle with a count; meaning is a side effect of pairs of words moving under a condition.

## 1. Our abstract

This is the thirteenth of sixteen papers in which Phient reads one work by Barbara Liskov through its own paradigm. The work is the keynote she gave at OOPSLA in October 1987, printed the following May in SIGPLAN Notices as "Data Abstraction and Hierarchy". It is eighteen pages, has no experiment and no measurement, and makes one argument: that object-oriented programming is at bottom data abstraction, that inheritance is an elaboration of it, and that inheritance is being used for two different things that ought to be kept apart. One thing is building an implementation out of another implementation. The other is saying that one type is a subtype of another. The paper's test for the second is a sentence about substitution, and that sentence is the origin of what the field later named the Liskov substitution principle.

We read it here because the quest in phiecs has a relation called is-a, and until now Phient has treated is-a as one channel among five, a coordinate on the relation circle where a word's phase is nudged toward its parent's. The paper says that is-a is a claim about programs. S is a subtype of T when any program written against T keeps its behaviour when handed an S. That is a claim Phient can state in its own terms, because its programs are operators, X ⇒ Y under c, and its behaviour is a number that the facts file records. Whether the quest's is-a satisfies the paper's test is not something Phient has measured, and section 12 proposes the measurement.

Sections 2 to 5 state what the paper says, from its full text, which we read. Sections 6 to 10 place it: the word as a data abstraction, the rep hierarchy of circle, sphere and helix as the paper's implementation hierarchy, the operator as the program P in the substitution sentence, the quest's is-a as the type hierarchy, and the paper's demand that the two be kept separate as an instruction phinet has half followed. Section 11 gives every number that bears on this, all from PHIENT_FACTS.md. Section 12 names the experiment and its control. Section 13 says where the reading breaks. Section 14 is the person and the year. Section 15 is what is left. Section 16 lists what we reached.

One limit up front. The paper is about language design and about how programmers should think. Phient trains its abstractions and does not write them. Every place below where the paper says a programmer decides, Phient has a loss and a trainer deciding. The analogy is stated with that gap kept in view.

## 2. What the work claims, in its terms

The abstract is four sentences. "Data abstraction is a valuable method for organizing programs to make them easier to modify and maintain. Inheritance allows one implementation of a data abstraction to be related to another hierarchically. This paper investigates the usefulness of hierarchy in program development, and concludes that although data abstraction is the more important idea, hierarchy does extend its usefulness in some situations."

The introduction sets the priority the rest of the paper keeps. "Object-oriented programming is primarily a data abstraction technique, and much of its power derives from this. However, it elaborates this technique with the notion of 'inheritance.' Inheritance can be used in a number of ways, some of which enhance the power of data abstraction." The paper's job is to sort those ways.

Section 2 of the paper restates data abstraction as she had stated it since 1974. "Thus a data abstraction is a set of objects that can be manipulated directly only by a set of operations." A specification goes with it: "The specification describes what the abstraction does, but omits any information about how it is implemented." The implementation is the rep: "The implementation of a data object is concerned with how that object is represented in the memory of a computer; this information is called the representation, or rep for short." The rep must be hidden. "For abstraction to work, implementations must be encapsulated. If an implementation is encapsulated, then no other module can depend on its implementation details." What this buys is locality. "Abstraction when supported by specifications and encapsulation provides locality within a program. Locality allows a program to be implemented, understood, or modified one module at a time."

Section 3 turns to inheritance, and here the paper's claim is the one it is remembered for. Inheritance is used in two ways. In the first, implementation hierarchy, a subclass reuses a superclass as a way of building its rep. In the second, type hierarchy, a subtype extends a supertype's behaviour. The paper says the first adds nothing: "Since implementation hierarchy does not allow us to do anything that we could not already do with data abstraction, we will not consider it further." The second it defines by a substitution property, quoted in full in section 3 below, and then spends sections 4 and 5 asking when a type hierarchy helps and what a language must provide for it.

The conclusion is measured. "We conclude that although data abstraction is more important, type hierarchy does extend its usefulness. Furthermore, inheritance is sometimes needed to express type hierarchy and is therefore a useful mechanism to provide in a programming language." Abstraction first, hierarchy second, the two uses kept apart.

## 3. The mechanism, plainly

The mechanism is a distinction and a test. The distinction is between two things a subclass can be. The test is the sentence that decides which one it is.

Take the distinction first. Section 3.2 of the paper, on implementation hierarchy, gives the example of integer sets. "Suppose we want to implement integer sets... Suppose further that a list data type has already been implemented, and that it provides a member operation and a size operation... Then we could implement set as a subclass of list; we might have the list hold the set elements without duplication." The paper points out that this is nothing inheritance was needed for: "Another way of doing the same thing is to use one (abstract) type as the rep of another. For example, we might implement sets by using list as the rep." A set is not a kind of list. It merely uses one. The hierarchy is an accident of construction and says nothing about what a set means.

Inheritance of this kind also costs something. Section 3.1 says that "there are three ways that encapsulation can be violated: the subclass might access an instance variable of its superclass, call a private operation of its superclass, or refer directly to superclasses of its superclass." Each makes the subclass depend on the superclass's rep.

Now the test. Section 3.3 defines type hierarchy by behaviour. A subtype is a type whose objects "provide all the behavior of objects of another type (the supertype) plus something extra." The condition is this: "If for each object o1 of type S there is an object o2 of type T such that for all programs P defined in terms of T, the behavior of P is unchanged when o1 is substituted for o2, then S is a subtype of T." Two consequences follow at once. "A subtype must have all the operations of its supertype since otherwise the using program could not use an operation it depends on." And having the operations is not enough: "The operations must also do the same things."

The paper is careful about the words. "Subtype" and "supertype" are semantic relations, facts about behaviour. "Subclass" and "superclass" are linguistic facts, about which class inherits from which. A subclass need not be a subtype, and the set-from-list is one that is not. Section 5 of the paper is about languages that cannot say a subtype relation that holds, and its diagnosis is one sentence: "The problem arises because inheritance is being used for two different things: to implement a type and to indicate that one type is a subtype of another. These uses should be kept separate."

## 4. What it demonstrated, as reported

The paper demonstrates by example, and the examples are chosen to show where the substitution test passes and where it fails. Stacks and queues fail it. "Stacks are LIFO; when an element is removed from a stack, the last item added (pushed) is removed. By contrast, queues are FIFO. A using program is likely to notice the difference between these two types." Both have insert and remove. Neither is a subtype of the other, because a program written for one behaves differently when given the other. Sets and lists fail it too, on the matter of duplicates. Having the same operations is not the test; behaving the same under a program is.

Indexed collections pass it. "All subtypes have these operations too, but, in addition, each would provide extra operations. Examples of subtypes are arrays, sequences, and indexed sets." Abstract devices pass it the same way: common operations such as testing for end of file, and device-specific extensions.

Section 4 of the paper reports three uses of type hierarchy. The first is incremental design. "Abstractions are usually developed incrementally", and as a design is refined "a single type may have several subtypes, representing the needs of different subparts of the program." If P depends on T, Q depends on S, and S is a subtype of T, a problem in S needs Q examined and not P. The second use is organizing related types. When the relation is known in advance, a supertype is the right tool. When it is discovered afterward, the paper offers an alternative: "An alternative approach is to simply allow the polymorphic module to use any type that supplies the needed operations. In this case no attempt is made to relate the types." The third use is a type library, where hierarchy helps a programmer browse.

Section 5 reports where languages of the day went wrong. In Smalltalk a class inheriting from a class of matrices inherits a rep. "Using inheritance in this way allows us to have several implementations of the same type in use within the same program, but it interferes with type hierarchy. For example, suppose we invent a subtype of matrices called extended-matrices. We would like to implement extended-matrices with a class that inherits from matrices rather than from a particular implementation of matrices." Sparse and non-sparse matrices are one type with two reps, and a subtype should inherit the type without committing to a rep. The languages did not permit it, and that is the paper's evidence that the two uses had been confused. The paper names Simula 67, CLU, Smalltalk, Ada, CommonLoops, Flavors, Trellis/Owl and Argus, and reports no benchmark or user study of any of them.

## 5. The one idea restated

Being a kind of something is a fact about programs and not about construction. S is a subtype of T if no program written for T can tell it has been given an S. That is the whole test, and it puts the burden where Liskov always put it: on behaviour as seen from outside, through operations, by a user who never sees the rep.

The idea has a negative half and a positive half. The negative half is that the way a thing is built tells you nothing about what it is. A set built from a list is not a list. A stack and a queue with the same operation names are not the same type. Inheritance, as the languages of 1987 offered it, recorded how things were built and let programmers mistake that record for a claim about meaning. The positive half is that there is a real relation called subtype, that it can be stated precisely, and that stating it lets a program be reasoned about one module at a time: a change to S is a change to the modules that use S, and the modules that use T can be left alone.

Restated for Phient, the sentence reads as follows. A word is a set of relations under conditions. A program in Phient is an operator, X ⇒ Y under c, applied to words and returning a comparison. Word s is a subtype of word t if, for every operator that was fit to t, the operator's behaviour is unchanged when s is put in t's place. That is is-a, and it is not a channel. It is a claim about every operator at once. The channel is where the quest stores its answer to the claim, as a phase. The claim itself is about what happens when the phase is used.

The paper's other sentence, that the two uses of inheritance should be kept separate, restates too. Phient has an implementation hierarchy: the circle tie, the sphere tie, the anisotropic tie, the helix's codons. Each is a rep for the same word, built from another rep, and the paper says correctly that this hierarchy tells us nothing about what a word means. Phient also has a type hierarchy, the quest's is-a, which is supposed to say what a word means. The paper's instruction is that the second must never be inferred from the first. That a word's phase under is-a sits near its parent's phase is a fact about a rep. Whether the word is a subtype is a fact about programs. Section 11 reports the one number that speaks to the gap, held-out is-a at two hops, and it is 0.095.

## 6. The word as a data abstraction, and the subtype as behaviour

Paper 4 in this series read the 1974 cluster as the word. The 1987 paper's section 2 is the 1974 definition restated for a Smalltalk audience. What it adds is the question of when two abstractions are related. Phient has been answering that by construction and not by test, and the paper is a reason to stop.

Consider what a word is in Phical. It is 32 to 64 relations, each a phasor on a closed circle with a count. Nothing outside the engine reads a phasor. The operators read words, and they return comparisons: better is a difference of two cosines, worse is a gap between a word's fit and its best continuation, loss is a hinge over better. So a word's behaviour is the set of comparisons every operator returns when the word is put in. That is the paper's sense of behaviour: what programs defined in terms of the type do when given an object of it.

Now put the substitution sentence into that vocabulary. Let T be a word and S another. For all operators fit in terms of T, meaning every prediction context in which T was the target or a candidate, is the operator's output unchanged when S stands in for T? If so, S is a subtype of T. The quest oracle asserts pairs of this kind under is-a. It asserts them as rows, and the trainer nudges S's phase toward T's on the is-a channel. What the trainer produces is a rep in which S and T are near. Whether the operators then treat S as a T is exactly the question the paper says the rep cannot answer.

There is a second reading, and it matters for the persona work. The three writers Chesterton, Austen and Carroll are held apart by attribution at 71% on held-out paragraphs, chance 33%. A persona is a word under a condition, and a paragraph is a program written in terms of one persona. Attribution succeeding means that substituting one writer into the other's program changes its behaviour, so the personas are not subtypes of each other. That is the stack and the queue: same operations, distinguishable under use. It is a substitution test that happens to have been run, and it came out negative, which is the right result for three writers.

The paper also gives Phient the word "extra". A subtype provides the supertype's behaviour "plus something extra." A child of T under is-a may have part-of and used-for relations T lacks. Whether the trained table supports that is unmeasured; the facts file records within-kind MRR 0.747 and says nothing about cross-kind inheritance of relations.

## 7. The relation channel as coordinate, and the two hierarchies

A channel in Phient is a coordinate on the relation circle. There are 32 or 64 of them, each a byte of 256 quanta of 2π. The paper's section 2 would call the byte table the rep, and its section 3.2 would call the ways of arranging it the implementation hierarchy. Phient has several. Tie::Sphere reads the 64 channels as spherical harmonics Y_l^m with l < 8, one complex weight per degree, 1,192 params. Tie::Anisotropic gives one weight per (l, |m|), 36 slots, 4,776 params. The circle tie has 8,360. The helix reads the same 64 channels as codons in base-4 ACGT and lays them on B-DNA geometry at 10.5 codons per turn with 0.34 nm rise, and writes 18,496 words in 1.9 s and 75 MB, byte-exact on the way back. PhiTon reads a codon as a wavelength, a band and a hex.

Each of these is built from the byte table the way the paper's set is built from the list. The sphere is a reweighting of the circle; the helix is a renaming of it; PhiTon is a renaming of the helix. This is an implementation hierarchy in the paper's exact sense, and its verdict applies: "Since implementation hierarchy does not allow us to do anything that we could not already do with data abstraction, we will not consider it further." The helix is a rep, and it is encapsulated: nothing that uses a word knows whether it came through the circle or the codons, and the byte-exact round trip is the proof.

The type hierarchy is elsewhere. It is in the quest, under the kind is-a, where 256 words stand in families of 8 and the oracle says which are children of which. It is not a rep decision. It is a claim about what the operators should do, and the trainer's job is to make a rep in which they do it. The scores are the report on whether the trainer succeeded: 0.594 MRR overall on the seen rows, within-kind 0.747, and on held-out rows one hop 0.011, two hops is-a 0.095.

The paper's three encapsulation violations have counterparts here. A subclass reading a superclass's instance variable is a caller reading a channel directly. A subclass calling a private operation is a caller using Tape::hinge by name instead of loss. A subclass referring to its grandparent is a caller who knows the sphere tie is underneath the circle. Docs/86 lists the second as what remains. If the quest's is-a were read as "child's byte table is derived from parent's", it would be an implementation hierarchy and would say nothing. The trainer does something close to that, and section 12 asks whether it is the right kind of nearness.

## 8. The operator as the program P

The substitution sentence has three variables: the object o1 of type S, the object o2 of type T, and the program P defined in terms of T. In Phient the objects are words and the program is the operator. This is the reading that makes the paper testable in phinet.

An operator X ⇒ Y under c is a function fit on training examples. On Austen it was fit on 204,592 examples over a pool of 1,228 words seen 20 or more times, and its behaviour is a ranking: given a context, it returns a prediction, and the prediction's cosine with each candidate is what better and worse compare. That operator is "defined in terms of" every word in the pool. It is the program P for all of them at once.

The paper's test then reads: substitute S for T in every context where T occurred, and ask whether the ranking is unchanged. Unchanged has to be defined, since the operator is continuous and no substitution leaves a cosine where it was. The natural definition uses the three operators. Substitution leaves P unchanged if better(pred, S, x) has the same sign as better(pred, T, x) for every candidate x: S and T are ranked against the same words the same way. A weaker definition is that loss(pred, S, decoy, margin) is within a tolerance of loss(pred, T, decoy, margin). Either is a number the engine can compute, and neither has been computed.

This reading says what is-a would have to be for the paper to accept it. The quest asks the operator, under kind is-a, to rank the parent first among 293 targets, and reports hit@1 34.8% and hit@5 97.4% on seen rows. That is P recognizing that S is related to T under c. It is not P being unable to tell S from T. The paper's subtype is invisible to P in T's contexts. The quest's child is visible to P as T's neighbour on one channel. The second relation does not imply the first.

The paper's other requirement is that "the operations must also do the same things." Every word in Phient has the same three operations, so having them is automatic. Doing the same things is the substitution test again. A word with better, worse and loss that returns different comparisons from its parent is a stack next to a queue.

One more correspondence, from the paper's section 4.1. A module Q using subtype S and a module P using supertype T can be examined separately. If a persona's words were subtypes of a corpus operator's words, a persona's failure could be examined in the persona's trainer alone. Phient has not organized its training that way.

## 9. Is-a in the quest as type hierarchy, and the hexagram as library

The quest was built as a synthetic oracle: 256 words in families of 8, five kinds, 1,280 rows, 293 target words, chance MRR 0.021. The five kinds are is-a, part-of, near, used-for and unlike. The paper speaks to exactly one of them, and speaks to it hard. Is-a is the only kind that the paper would call a type hierarchy. The other four are relations between types that the paper's section 4.2 would call related types, to be handled by grouping and not by hierarchy. Part-of is the paper's set-uses-list: a rep relation. Near is a similarity that carries no substitution promise. Used-for and unlike say nothing about programs at all.

That the quest treats all five identically, as a nudge on a channel, is the confusion of construction with meaning the paper warns against. A trainer that puts a child near its parent under is-a is doing what a Smalltalk class does when it inherits a rep. What the paper wants is a test that the child behaves as the parent under all programs, and the quest has none. Its held-out numbers are the nearest thing: two-hop is-a at 0.095 says that, given a child and a grandparent the trainer never saw together, the operator ranks the grandparent above chance but far below the seen rows. If is-a were a type hierarchy in the paper's sense, two hops would be free, since a subtype of a subtype is a subtype by substitution twice. The channel encodes nearness; it does not encode transitivity.

The paper's third use of hierarchy, organizing a type library, has a counterpart Phient did build. Docs/86 files each quest word under the hexagram its 64-channel function is, and 256 words occupy 61 of 64 hexagrams. That is a library index. The paper says a hierarchy helps a programmer browse "without requiring inheritance mechanisms", and the hexagram filing is exactly that: a place to look, not a claim that words in the same hexagram substitute for each other. The paper would approve of the modesty and would ask whether the index is any use. The three operators averaged over the filing say the specification is too flat to tell: mean better −0.008, worse −0.008, loss 0.108 at margin 0.1.

The paper's section 4.2 alternative reads against the quest too. "An alternative approach is to simply allow the polymorphic module to use any type that supplies the needed operations." Every Phient word supplies the same three operations, so every operator is already polymorphic over every word, and "no attempt is made to relate the types." The quest's families are imposed from outside by the oracle. Whether the operator discovers families it was not told about is a question for a Phi-4 run, which has not been run.

## 10. Where the idea lands: two uses, kept separate

The paper's practical instruction is that a language should let a programmer say "S is a subtype of T" without saying "S is built from T's rep", and say "S is built from T's rep" without saying anything about subtypes. Phient has both kinds of statement and they land in different crates.

Rep inheritance lands in phinet. Tie::Sphere and Tie::Anisotropic are built over the circle's 64 channels; the helix over the byte table; PhiTon over the helix. None claims a word means anything different. The paper is satisfied here, because the trainer chooses a tie and no caller sees which. The costs show up in the engine, allocation and bandwidth bound with a 1 MB op at 2 to 5 ms, and in the scores: anisotropic 16.0× valid against circle 14.7×, sphere 12.8×. Three reps, one type.

Type hierarchy lands in phiecs, in the quest, under is-a. Here the paper is not satisfied, because the only thing that says S is-a T is a row in the oracle and a nudge on a channel. There is no program P and no check that P is unchanged. The shipped trainer's 0.082 against attraction-only's 0.594 reports that the mechanism was wrong once and was fixed; neither number is a substitution test.

The place where the two uses risk mixing is the helix, which files the lexicon as a tree of codons. The codon is a rep of the channel, so the tree is an implementation hierarchy. If it were read as saying that words sharing a prefix are subtypes, that would be the paper's confusion in its purest form. Docs/84 does not make that claim, and this paper is a reason never to.

The three operators land as the operations of the supertype. Every Phient word has better, worse and loss, so a subtype relation among words can only be about whether those three do the same things for S as for T. That reduces the paper's test to three inequalities per context, and docs/86's antisymmetry test on better is the only one phinet checks today.

Forcing lands as a program P that could run the test. forcing_choice picks the largest cos − λ·worse among candidates. At λ = 0 it is greedy. If a child word is a true subtype of its parent, forcing should pick the same continuation after either. On text, forcing at λ = 1 raised the evaluator to 0.712 and perplexity to 107.4, so the two programs disagree about which continuation is better. Under the paper, a program pair that disagrees about the same objects is the sign that one of them is not defined in terms of the type the other is. Phient does not know which.

## 12. The experiment, with its control

The paper's test is a sentence with a quantifier over programs. Phient can approximate the quantifier with the operators it has. The experiment asks whether the quest's is-a pairs pass the substitution test, and whether pairs under the other four kinds fail it, which the paper says they should.

Setup. Take the quest as trained with attraction-only and dithered rounding, 0.594 MRR. Take every training row of the form (S is-a T). For each such pair, collect every context in which T appears as the target in the operator's training set. Substitute S for T in those contexts and run the operator unchanged. Record two things per context: whether the sign of better(pred, x, y) is the same for every candidate pair (x, y) in the pool before and after the substitution, and the change in loss(pred, target, decoy, 0.1) against the same decoy. Average over contexts and over pairs. Call this the is-a substitution score.

Claim under test. If the quest's is-a is a type hierarchy in the paper's sense, the substitution score for is-a pairs should be close to the score for substituting T for itself, the ceiling, and far above arbitrary pairs. If it is only nearness on one channel, the is-a score will sit near the score for near pairs.

Control. Three. First, the same procedure over near pairs, which the paper says are related types without a substitution promise; is-a should beat near. Second, over unlike pairs, which give the floor. Third, over random pairs matched on count, which controls for the operator not caring which word is present. A fourth arm, reversed is-a, substituting T for S in S's contexts, tests direction: a subtype may stand in for its supertype but not the reverse.

Second arm, transitivity. Repeat for held-out two-hop pairs, where the oracle asserts S is-a T and T is-a U but the trainer saw only one. The facts file's 0.095 at two hops is a ranking score; the substitution score asks directly whether U's contexts are unchanged by S.

What would count. Is-a within 10% of the self-substitution ceiling on sign agreement, above near by more than near is above random, and the reversed arm lower than the forward one. Anything else says the quest's is-a is an implementation hierarchy, in the paper's words a set built from a list, and the next step is a loss that penalizes changes in the operator's behaviour rather than distance on a channel. Not run. The cost is one pass of the operator over the contexts of 293 target words, well inside the engine's 2 to 5 ms per 1 MB op.

## 13. Where we disagree, or the analogy breaks

The first break is the quantifier. The paper says "for all programs P defined in terms of T." Phient has one operator per training run and the programs that call it, and "for all" cannot be checked, only sampled. Section 12 samples it over contexts. The paper's relation is a universal claim, and Phient's approximation is a statistic. A word that substitutes in 95% of contexts is not a subtype in the paper's sense. It is a word that usually works, and the paper was written because usually is not a specification.

The second is that Phient's types are not distinct. The paper's stacks and queues are different types with different specifications. Every Phient word is the same type, the word, with the same three operations, and the quest's is-a is a relation between objects of one type. The paper's subtype is a relation between types. Reading is-a as subtyping asks the substitution sentence to do work at the object level that Liskov defined at the type level. The reading is useful, and it is a stretch, and it should be called one.

The third is behaviour as a number. The paper's "behavior of P is unchanged" is exact: the program does the same thing. Phient's operators are continuous, and no substitution leaves a cosine unchanged. Section 12 chose sign agreement on better as the discrete surrogate. That is a choice, and the paper does not endorse it. A different surrogate, such as the ranking within hit@10, would give a different score, and the paper gives no principle for choosing.

The fourth is the direction of the hierarchy. The paper's subtype has the supertype's behaviour "plus something extra." The quest's child has the parent's phase on one channel, and whether it has anything extra is not something the trainer is asked for. The extra, in the paper, is what makes the subtype worth having. Phient's is-a is at best the first half.

The fifth is who decides. The paper is about a designer choosing when to declare a subtype and a language that lets the designer say so. Phient's trainer declares nothing; the oracle's rows are the only declaration in the system. The paper's advice to provide two mechanisms and keep them separate translates for Phient into advice to provide two losses. That is a translation, and the paper does not make it.

Last, the paper's conclusion is modest. Hierarchy "does extend" the usefulness of abstraction "in some situations." Phient's quest is built on five kinds of hierarchy and treats them all alike. The paper would say four of the five are not hierarchies and the fifth has not been tested. We agree, and that is what section 12 is for.

## 14. The person and the moment

Barbara Liskov, born Barbara Huberman, took her PhD at Stanford in 1968 under John McCarthy, worked at MITRE, and joined the MIT faculty in 1972. By 1987 she had led the CLU project, published the 1974 abstract data type paper with Zilles, the 1977 CACM paper on CLU's abstraction mechanisms and the 1978 reference manual, and had turned to distributed computing with Argus, which the 1987 paper names among the languages it discusses. The paper's header records that the research was supported by the NEC Professorship of Software Science and Engineering, which she held at MIT.

The moment was the second OOPSLA, held in Orlando, Florida, on 4 to 8 October 1987, according to the dblp and researchr conference records we reached. Object-oriented programming was arriving as a movement, with Smalltalk-80 in wide use, C++ new, and the Lisp object systems CommonLoops and Flavors, all of which the paper names. Liskov gave the keynote. A keynote from the person who had defined abstract data types thirteen years earlier, to a community that believed inheritance was the central idea, was an occasion, and the paper's opening sentence is the whole of her position: object-oriented programming "is primarily a data abstraction technique." The paper was printed the following May, in the Addendum to the OOPSLA '87 Proceedings, as ACM SIGPLAN Notices volume 23, number 5, pages 17 to 34, under the title "Keynote address - data abstraction and hierarchy." The acknowledgments thank several people for help and "especially the efforts of Toby Bloom and Gary Leavens."

The paper does not use the phrase "substitution principle" and does not name the property after its author. The naming came later, from others. The formal development of the idea is the 1994 paper with Jeannette Wing, "A Behavioral Notion of Subtyping", in ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems, which restates the property as a claim about provable properties: a property provable of objects of type T should be true of objects of any subtype S. The 1994 paper adds the requirements on preconditions, postconditions and invariants, and a history constraint on state changes, which Wikipedia identifies as the novel element of that paper. None of that is in the 1987 keynote. What the keynote has is the sentence, the two hierarchies, and the instruction to keep them apart.

The 2008 Turing Award citation names data abstraction first among her contributions. Everything in this section is from the paper's own text, the conference records named in section 16 and Wikipedia's article on the substitution principle. We have not consulted a biography and state nothing about the year beyond these.

## 15. What remains

The paper leaves Phient one instruction and two questions.

The instruction is to keep the two hierarchies apart in the code and in the writing. The rep hierarchy, circle to sphere to helix to PhiTon, is an implementation hierarchy and should never be read as saying what a word means. The quest's is-a is intended as a type hierarchy and should never be built as a rep relation. Today it is built as one: a nudge on a channel toward the parent's phase. The paper's diagnosis of Smalltalk applies to the trainer without change: it is "being used for two different things", and the fix is to give is-a its own test.

The first question is section 12's: does the quest's is-a pass the substitution test at all, and does it pass it more than near does? The experiment is one operator pass over the contexts of 293 targets and has not been run. Its result decides whether the quest is a type hierarchy or a set built from a list.

The second question is what a loss for substitution looks like. The three operators are better, worse and loss, and loss is a hinge over better on one context. A substitution loss would be a penalty on the change in better's sign across all of T's contexts when S is substituted. That is a fourth operator or a new use of the third, and docs/86 did not anticipate it. Whether it can be trained, and whether it would cost the quest's within-kind score of 0.747, is unknown.

Beyond these, the paper's section 4.1 suggests a way to organize training that Phient has not tried: fit the operator on supertypes first, then fit subtypes against contexts the supertype operator leaves unchanged. The persona work is the natural place. Nothing in the facts file measures a persona trained this way.

The paper's section 4.2 suggests the opposite discipline for the other four kinds: do not relate them by hierarchy, and let the polymorphic operator take any word. The question is whether the four kinds then need channels at all. The Phi-4 quest, unrun, is where that would be tested.

Last, transitivity. Two-hop is-a at 0.095 is the one number Phient has about composition of the relation, and it is low. The paper's subtype composes by definition. If Phient's does not, no amount of nearness on a channel will make is-a a type hierarchy, and the byte ceiling that docs/86 reports on the hexagram filing is the likely reason. That is the rep leaking into the type, which is the paper's third encapsulation violation, and it is the one Phient has least idea how to close.

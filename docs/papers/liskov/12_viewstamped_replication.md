# The viewstamp as a relation: a view as a channel, a majority as a condition, a lost event as loss

*Phient interpreted papers · Liskov series, 12 of 16 · 2026-06-15 · on: "Viewstamped Replication: A New Primary Copy Method to Support Highly-Available Distributed Systems" (Brian M. Oki and Barbara H. Liskov, Proceedings of the ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing, 1988, pp. 8–17)*

"In Phient's world, a word (w) is nothing but what it relates to, under what conditions (c). The Phical paradigm, X ⇒ Y, drives and composes Phient's Phasor Language Models: Phinet, Phiano, PhiTon. Phical stores no point: a word is 32–64 relations, each a complex phasor on a closed circle with a count; meaning is a side effect of pairs of words moving under a condition."

## 1. Our abstract

The 1988 paper by Oki and Liskov is ten pages long and describes one algorithm. A module is copied onto several nodes. One copy, the primary, does the work. The others, the backups, are told what the primary did. When the primary fails, or the network splits, the survivors form a new view, pick a new primary, and go on. The paper's contribution is a small piece of bookkeeping that makes this safe: a viewstamp, which is a timestamp joined to the identity of the view in which it was issued. A viewstamp says not only when an event happened but under which arrangement of the group it happened. With that one datum, a cohort can tell how much another cohort knows, and a transaction can tell whether the work it did survived.

This paper reads the algorithm through Phient. The reading rests on the shape of the viewstamp. A viewstamp is a pair, a viewid and a timestamp, and the timestamp means nothing outside its view. A Phical relation is also a pair, a channel and a phase, and the phase means nothing outside its channel. The quest measured that: within-kind MRR 0.747, wrong channel 0.015, offset reading 0.005, chance 0.021. So the view reads as the condition c, and the viewstamp reads as the relation. The primary reads as the operator, the one place where X ⇒ Y is computed, and the backups read as the count, the record of how many times the group has seen the event. The rule that an event is safe only once a majority knows it reads as Phient's byte deadband: a nudge that reaches fewer than half a quantum is an event that was never forced to a majority, and it is lost at the next rounding, as the paper says information is lost at a view change.

The three operators are in the view change. The new primary is the cohort with the largest viewstamp, which is better. A cohort that accepted from a crash, with its state gone, is one that leads nowhere, which is worse. A transaction whose prepare finds its history incompatible is refused, which is loss. Section 11 tables the measured numbers, all from the facts file. Section 12 proposes majority-gated training with the shipped dithered trainer as control. Section 13 says where the analogy breaks, chiefly that Liskov's cohorts hold the same state and Phient's channels hold different states. Section 16 lists the sources reached: the paper itself, via a course mirror, and the 2012 revisit by abstract and secondary account.

## 2. What the work claims, in its terms

The abstract states the setting in one sentence: "One of the potential benefits of distributed systems is their use in providing highly-available services that are likely to be usable when needed. Availability is achieved through replication." The claim is that a known technique, primary copy, can be made to work in a general network. In the primary copy technique, "computations run at a primary, which notifies its backups of what it has done." The paper's method "works in a general network with both node crashes and partitions." The abstract adds what is new: "little delay in user computations and little information is lost in a reorganization; we use a special kind of timestamp called a viewstamp to detect lost information."

The failure model is stated with care. Nodes "can crash" and "are fail-stop processors." The network "may lose, delay, and duplicate messages, or deliver messages out of order." Partitions are allowed. The paper says the original primary copy scheme of Alsberg and Day "worked only if node failures were distinguishable from network failures; in general such a distinction cannot be made."

The computational model is Liskov's own from Argus. Programs are "modules, each of which resides at a single node," and they "communicate by means of remote procedure calls." Work is done in transactions, and the correctness criterion is "one-copy serializability." The paper describes the algorithm for a system without nested transactions and says that "an algorithm for our method in a system with nested transactions is described in [32]; it is based on the implementation of nested transactions in Argus." Reference 32 is Oki's dissertation.

The paper's own summary of what it achieved is in its conclusions: "This paper has described a new replication method for providing high availability. The method performs well in the normal case, does view changes efficiently, and loses little information in a view change." And on the viewstamp: "Viewstamps allows us to determine inexpensively how much each cohort knows and whether a transaction can be committed." The paper calls the design "an interesting compromise between loss of work in failures and extra information." It does not promise that no work is lost. It promises that lost work is detected and small, in exchange for a little extra state.

The related work section compares against voting, the virtual partitions protocol, ISIS, and the single-backup systems of Tandem and Auragen. Against voting: "Our method is faster than voting for write operations since we require fewer messages." Against virtual partitions: the paper's "view change protocol is a simplification and modification of this protocol." Against one backup: those systems "can survive only a single failure."

## 3. The mechanism, plainly

Take the parts in the paper's order.

A module group. "A module group consists of several copies of the module, called cohorts, which behave as a single, logical entity." The set of all cohorts is the configuration. "One cohort is designated as the primary; it executes procedure calls, and participates in two-phase commit. The remaining cohorts are backups, which are essentially passive and merely receive state information from the primary."

A view. "A view is a set of cohorts that are (or were) capable of communicating with each other, together with an indication of which cohort is the primary; it is a subset of the configuration and must contain a majority of group members." A view has a viewid.

An event and a viewstamp. "An event record identifies the type of the event, and contains other relevant information about the event." Events go into a communication buffer, and "information in the buffer is sent to the backups in timestamp order." "Each event is assigned a unique timestamp, and later events receive later timestamps." Then the definition: "A viewstamp is simply a timestamp concatenated with the viewid of the view in which the timestamp was generated." Together they name an event without ambiguity, because timestamps from different views are never compared as numbers.

Forcing. Normally the primary sends the buffer lazily. At commit it forces: it sends and waits until a majority acknowledges. "The coordinator adds a 'committing' record to its buffer and forces the entire buffer to the backups. This ensures that the commit will be known across a view change of the coordinator." Commit needs "all its events are known to at least a majority of cohorts."

History and the check at prepare. Each cohort keeps a history, "a sequence of viewstamps, each with a different viewid," one per view it has been in, and the rule is that "a cohort's state reflects event e from view v.id iff e's timestamp is less than or equal to v.ts." Each remote call is stamped: "Each time a server finishes processing a remote call on behalf of a transaction, it assigns the call a viewstamp." The transaction carries the set of these, the pset. At prepare, a participant "can agree to prepare only if it knows about all remote calls its group has done on behalf of the preparing transaction. It uses its history and the pset in the prepare message to determine this." And: "If the pset is not compatible with the history, it refuses the prepare."

That is the whole method. Work at one place. Tell the others in order. Force at commit. Stamp every call. Refuse to commit anything whose stamps the group no longer covers.

## 4. What it demonstrated, as reported

The paper is an algorithm paper. What it demonstrates is a set of properties argued from the protocol, and the view change is where the argument lives.

The invariant is stated plainly: "If every view has at least a majority of cohorts, then it contains at least one cohort that knows about any event that was forced to a majority of cohorts." Two majorities must overlap, so some member of every later view holds the committed events. The view change protocol's task is to find that member and start the new view from its state, so that "events known to a majority of cohorts survive into subsequent views" and each new view begins in the "latest possible state."

The protocol runs like this. A cohort that suspects a failure becomes view manager. It "creates a new viewid by pairing mymid with a number greater than max-viewid.cnt," then "sends invitations to join the new view to all other cohorts, and waits for responses." Each response is an acceptance of one of two kinds. A "normal" acceptance carries the cohort's current viewstamp and which cohort it thinks is primary. A "crash-accept" says the cohort has recovered from a crash and its state is gone. The manager may form the view under three conditions: "a majority of cohorts accepted normally"; "crash-viewid < normal-viewid," meaning no crashed cohort was in a view later than the best normal one; or "crash-viewid = normal-viewid and the primary of view normal-viewid has done a normal acceptance." Then the primary is chosen: "The cohort returning the largest viewstamp (in a 'normal' acceptance) is selected as the new primary; the old primary of that view is selected if possible." A recovering cohort "initializes up-to-date to be false" and "initializes status to be 'view-manager'; this causes it to start a view change," so that it rejoins by the same protocol everyone else uses.

What the paper reports as the result is a comparison, not a measurement. Against voting it claims fewer messages on writes. Against ISIS, where "calls are sent to a single cohort" and a read "acquires a read lock locally and performs the operation locally," it observes that ISIS's information "cannot be discarded when transactions commit," whereas its own buffer can be. No implementation is timed and no experiment is reported. The paper reached here contains no numbers about throughput, latency or availability. "Little delay" is a claim about message counts in the normal case. "Little information lost" is a claim about the protocol: what was forced survives, what was not forced may be lost, and the viewstamp says which.

## 5. The one idea restated

An event is not safe when it happens. It is safe when a majority knows it. And to know whether a majority knows it, name the event by when it happened and under whom.

The first half is older than the paper. It is the majority rule of voting and of the virtual partitions protocol, and the paper credits both. The second half is the paper's. A timestamp alone cannot do it, because after a reorganization a new primary issues new timestamps and there is no telling which of two stamps is later or whether the earlier one's event survived. A viewid alone cannot do it, because within a view events must be ordered. The viewstamp is both, and its use is a comparison. A cohort's history is a list of the highest viewstamp it holds for each view. A transaction's pset is a list of the viewstamps of its calls. Compatibility is a check that each stamp in the pset is covered by the history. If it is, the work survived. If not, the work was in an unforced part of a buffer at a view that ended, and it is gone, and the transaction is refused.

The idea has a cost the paper accepts openly. Work done between forces may be lost. The paper does not try to prevent this. It makes it detectable and bounds it, and calls the result a compromise between lost work and extra information. That stance is the same as the one in Liskov's earlier work on exceptions: do not pretend the failure did not happen, name it and let the caller decide.

Restated for Phient: a relation is a phase on a channel, and a nudge to a relation is an event. The nudge is not safe when it is computed. It is safe when it has been written into the byte, and the byte deadband is the exact place where an event fails to reach a majority. A nudge of 0.06·sin d falls under half a quantum once sin d < 0.2, about 11 degrees. Nearest rounding froze every pair 8 quanta short. Those are events that happened at the primary and were never forced. Phient had no viewstamp to say so, and found out by measuring the quest at 0.082 MRR. Sections 6 through 10 take the pieces in turn: the group and the word, the view and the channel, the primary and the operator, the view change and the three operators, the backup and the count.

## 6. The word as a function, and the module group

"A module group consists of several copies of the module, called cohorts, which behave as a single, logical entity." The entity the caller sees is not any cohort. It is what the group answers. A call goes to the primary, and the answer is the group's answer because the primary's events will be known to a majority before anything depends on them.

In Phical a word is 32–64 relations and nothing else, and its meaning is what pairs do under a condition. The word is a function from (condition, partner) to a comparison. Read through the paper, the word is a module group. No relation is the word. The word is the single logical entity that the relations, taken together, present to a caller. And the caller is always an operator: better(pred, a, b), worse(pred, a, after, continuations), loss(pred, target, decoy, margin). Those are the remote procedure calls. A word answers only through the operator, the way a group answers only through its primary.

The paper's cohorts are copies, and section 13 takes that up. But there is a reading on which it holds. The Austen pool of 1,228 words seen 20 or more times, with 204,592 training examples, presents each word many times, in many contexts, and each presentation is a cohort's view of the same word. The trainer's job is to make them behave as one entity. The measure of whether they do is the operator's MRR against chance 0.0063: identity 7.9× chance, hinge at 8,360 params 14.7× valid and 14.6× test, softmax β=8 18.0× and 18.8×, softmax wide+deep at 50,016 params 27.6× at 3 epochs and 30.7×/30.3× at 8 epochs, hit@10 0.348. A word whose contexts disagree is a group whose cohorts have diverged.

The other half of the paper's definition of a group is the configuration, the fixed set of cohorts from which every view must draw a majority. For a Phient word that set is the 64 channels. A view is a subset; a word under one condition uses one subset of its channels and a word under another condition uses another. The requirement that a view be a majority has no measured analogue in Phient and section 15 lists it as open. But the shape is right. The persona work shows a word under three configurations at once: three writers, Chesterton, Austen and Carroll, held-out 4-sentence paragraphs attributed at 71% overall (74/65/86) against chance 33%. The same lexicon, three groups, three primaries. The attribution number is how well the groups can be told apart by their answers, which is the only way a caller can ever tell one module group from another.

## 7. The relation channel as a coordinate, and the view

"A viewstamp is simply a timestamp concatenated with the viewid of the view in which the timestamp was generated." The contribution is in the concatenation. A timestamp orders events, but only within one view. The viewid is the coordinate that says which ordering the number belongs to.

A Phical relation has the same form. It is a phase, a byte of 256 quanta of 2π, joined to a channel. A phase orders nothing outside its own circle. The channel is the coordinate. So a relation is a viewstamp, with the channel where the viewid goes and the phase where the timestamp goes.

The quest measured the coordinate dropped. On the synthetic oracle, 256 words in families of 8 with kinds is-a, part-of, near, used-for and unlike, 1,280 rows, 293 target words, chance MRR 0.021: reading the relation on its own kind's channel gives 0.747; reading it on the wrong channel gives 0.015; reading at an offset gives 0.005. Below chance both times. That is the paper's rule that a timestamp without its view means nothing. Phient found it as a measurement; the paper states it as a definition.

The view is the condition c in one more respect. A view is "a set of cohorts that are (or were) capable of communicating with each other, together with an indication of which cohort is the primary." Under condition c, some relations of a word are in play and others are not, and one of them, the one the operator reads first, is the primary. A view begins at a view change and ends at the next. A change of c is a reorganization of which relations are live. The helix writes the coordinate a second way. Each channel is a codon, 64 codons as base-4 ACGT of the channel, with hexagram, sphere degree and order, B-DNA xyz at 10.5 codons per turn and 0.34 nm rise, and a PhiTon wavelength, band and hex per codon. The codon is the viewid written four ways. 18,496 words in 1.9 s, 75 MB, byte-exact on the round trip. Byte-exact is what the paper needs of a viewid: after a reorganization the coordinate must come back the same.

The sphere tie changes the coordinate's shape and not its role. Under phinet::Tie::Sphere the 64 channels are Y_l^m with l < 8, one complex weight per degree, 1,192 params. The viewid becomes a pair (l, m). The anisotropic tie, one weight per (l, |m|), 36 slots, 4,776 params, gives 16.0× valid / 15.6× test on Austen hinge at 3 epochs against the circle's 14.7×/14.6× at 8,360 params. The coordinate system changed; the requirement that every phase carry one did not.

## 8. The operator as X ⇒ Y under c, and the primary

"One cohort is designated as the primary; it executes procedure calls, and participates in two-phase commit." Everything runs at one place. The backups "merely receive state information from the primary." The reason is speed. The primary answers, and the group catches up.

X ⇒ Y under c is that call. The operator takes a word X and a condition c and returns a ranking over Y. It runs at one place: the tensor [b,w,s] of complex phasors, one FFT, one comparison, one result. Nothing else runs it. The trainer runs it once and pushes the nudges out. That is the buffer, and the nudges are event records: "an event record identifies the type of the event, and contains other relevant information about the event." A nudge names a channel, a direction and a size. The buffer is sent "in timestamp order," and the tape's backward pass is in order too.

The paper's distinction between sending and forcing is the part of the reading that bites. Sending is lazy. The primary answers the caller and the backups learn of the event later. Forcing waits for a majority. Commit forces. In Phient, applying a nudge to a float is sending. Rounding the float to the byte is forcing. A nudge that does not move the byte was sent and never forced. It is in the buffer of a view that will end at the next rounding, and it is lost. The facts file gives the size of the loss: nudge 0.06·sin d falls under half a quantum once sin d < 0.2, and nearest rounding froze every pair 8 quanta short. The shipped trainer, which rounded nearest, reached 0.082 MRR on the quest. The attraction-only trainer with dithered rounding reached 0.594, hit@1 34.8%, hit@5 97.4%. Dithered rounding is a way of forcing: it makes a small nudge reach the byte some fraction of the time, so that a majority of presentations eventually carries it. It costs corpus attribution 71% to 65%, so it ships off by default. That is the paper's compromise between lost work and extra information.

Decoding is the normal case. On 40 held-out prompts × 30 tokens, the trigram sampler gives KN perplexity 13.3, operator nearest-k 66.9 (was 97.2), union 66.8. Shared 4-grams across prompts are 25.7% for the sampler and 8.7% for the operator. Each token is one call at the primary, answered without waiting, which is "little delay in user computations." The engine is allocation and bandwidth bound at these sizes, 1 MB op in 2–5 ms, which says the cost is in moving the buffer, not in the call.

## 9. The three operators, and the view change

The view change is where the paper compares. It is a decision made by a group under failure, and it is made by comparison and not by score. That is the shape of Liskov's 1968 endgame program, and Phient's three operators, phinet::liskov better, worse and loss, read straight into it.

better. "The cohort returning the largest viewstamp (in a 'normal' acceptance) is selected as the new primary." The manager computes no fitness. It compares viewstamps, and the largest is the one no other is better than. In phinet, better(pred, a, b) is cos(pred, a) − cos(pred, b), antisymmetric, with a unit test for it. The new primary is the candidate with the largest better against every other. On the quest, better is the truth's margin over the best wrong word on the kind's channel. The mean is −0.008. The truth and the best wrong word return the same viewstamp on almost every row. The view manager has a tie.

worse. A "crash-accept" is a cohort saying it has lost its state. The three formation rules keep such a cohort from becoming primary or being counted as if it knew things it does not. A crashed cohort is a move that leads nowhere: whatever its stamp, nothing continues from it. worse(pred, a, after, continuations) is cos(pred, a) − max over continuations of cos(after, t), how far the best onward reading falls below a's own fit, and its unit test is that a dead-end move is refuted. On the quest, mean worse is −0.008. On text, forcing_choice with cos − λ·worse at k = 32 gives KN 69.7 at λ = 0.05, 68.5 at λ = 0.25, 107.4 at λ = 1, against nearest-k 66.9, with the evaluator at 0.712, distinct 0.777 and shared 4-grams 22.3% at λ = 1. Forcing does not beat greedy on text. Every word in text has some continuation, so no candidate ever crash-accepts. The paper's rule has no work to do where nothing crashes.

loss. "If the pset is not compatible with the history, it refuses the prepare." The transaction aborts. Nothing partial commits. loss(pred, target, decoy, margin) is max(0, margin − better), the hinge, and on the quest its mean is 0.108 at margin 0.1: the margin is not met on nearly every row, so nearly every row is refused. The facts file calls this flat, the byte ceiling seen through the third operator; the 256 words fill 61 of 64 hexagrams. The paper says what a flat table of refusals means. The pset is not covered by the history. Events were sent and never forced. That is a diagnosis, and it points at rounding and not at the operators.

## 10. The count as the backup, and phiecs as the configuration

The backups "are essentially passive and merely receive state information from the primary." Their only active moment is the view change, when each reports its viewstamp and the manager counts how many accepted normally. The count is what a majority is made of.

Each Phical relation is a phasor and a count. The phasor is the primary's state, the thing the operator reads. The count is the backup: how many times the group has been told about this relation. It does not enter better, worse or loss. It is read at the view change, which in Phient is rounding: whether a nudge is committed to the byte depends on how much has accumulated, and the count is that accumulation's record. A high count has been forced many times over; a low count may sit in a buffer the next reorganization discards. The paper's rule for what survives a view change is the rule for what survives rounding, and the count is the datum the rule is applied to.

phiecs is where the configuration lives. It is the entity-component world, and the quest is its ingest. bin/quest_ingest scores every word by the three operators and files it under the hexagram its 64-channel function is, the centroid phase in 64 sectors. In the paper's terms the entity is the module, the components are its cohorts, and the ingest is the primary running calls on behalf of a transaction, one row of the oracle at a time. The prepare is the held-out test. Held-out 20%: one hop 0.011 MRR; two hops is-a 0.095, used-for 0.093, part-of, near and unlike at chance. One hop is refused almost always. Two hops under two kinds commit sometimes. The paper says why a two-hop transaction can commit where a one-hop cannot: it did more calls, and its pset covers more of the history, and where the history is thin, more stamps give the check more to find.

The helix is the backups' store. When a view ends, the new primary's state must be what the group agreed on, and it must come back exactly. 18,496 words to 75 MB in 1.9 s and byte-exact on the round trip is the property. A word read back from the helix is a recovered cohort that needs no view change, because the coordinate came back with the phase.

One more thing the paper gives phiecs. The primary is chosen from the acceptances, and the old primary is kept "if possible." Phient has no rule for which relation of a word the operator reads first, and no notion of keeping it stable across retraining. Section 15 lists that.

## 12. The experiment: majority-gated commit, with the dithered trainer as control

The paper's rule is that an event commits when a majority of cohorts knows it. Phient's rule is that a nudge commits when it moves the byte, and dither makes that happen by chance. The experiment replaces chance with a majority.

Setup. Take the quest oracle as it stands: 256 words, families of 8, five kinds, 1,280 rows, 293 targets, chance MRR 0.021. Run the attraction-only trainer as k cohorts, k = 3 and k = 5, each a separate pass over the same rows in a different order, each keeping its own float accumulator per relation and never rounding on its own. One cohort is primary and produces the nudges; all cohorts receive them. A nudge is committed to the shared byte only when a majority of cohorts' accumulators agree on the direction of the move by at least half a quantum. That is forcing. Nudges that never reach a majority are dropped at the end of each epoch, which is the view change, and their count is logged.

Control. The shipped attraction-only trainer with dithered rounding, 0.594 MRR, hit@1 34.8%, hit@5 97.4%, within-kind 0.747, wrong channel 0.015. A second control is the same trainer with nearest rounding and no dither, 0.082, which is the paper's primary copy with no forcing at all.

Measures. Quest MRR, hit@1, hit@5, within-kind and wrong-channel readings, all against the two controls. Mean better, worse and loss at margin 0.1, and the hexagram occupancy out of 64, against the flat −0.008, −0.008, 0.108 and 61 of 64. The number of nudges dropped per epoch, which is the paper's "information lost in a reorganization" as a count. Corpus attribution on the three writers against 71% and 65%, because dither's cost is the reason it ships off, and a majority gate that did not pay it would be a different compromise.

View change. After training, swap the primary: pick a different cohort's accumulator as the source and run one more epoch. Measure whether the byte lexicon is unchanged, which it should be if every committed nudge was known to a majority, and how many nudges the new primary tries to reissue, which is what the viewstamp check would have refused.

Prediction, stated as a prediction and not a result. If the deadband is the whole story, majority gating should reach the dithered number or better, and drop dither's cost to attribution because it commits by agreement and not by noise. If it does not reach 0.594, the loss is not only in when nudges commit, and the paper's compromise is not the right lens. Either result is worth having. Nothing here has been run.

## 13. Where the analogy breaks

The paper's cohorts are copies. "Several copies of the module." Every backup is meant to hold the same state as the primary, and the view change succeeds because any majority contains a cohort whose state is a superset of what committed. Phient's channels are not copies. They are 64 different relations under 64 different conditions, and the whole point of the coordinate is that a phase on one is not comparable to a phase on another. A majority of channels agreeing means nothing, because they are not asked the same question. Section 12 built cohorts out of training passes rather than channels for this reason. The paper's group is homogeneous. Phient's word is not.

The paper's timestamp is a total order. Later events get later stamps, and the history check is a comparison of numbers. A phase is a point on a circle. It has no later and no earlier; it has a direction and a distance, and the distance wraps. The check that makes the paper work, the pset against the history, has no direct analogue, and the rounding reading in sections 8 and 9 is a reading and not a mapping.

The paper's failures are crashes and partitions. Phient's losses are in arithmetic. A nudge under half a quantum is not a crash. Nothing failed; the byte simply cannot represent the move. The paper detects loss because it is caused by an identifiable event, a view change, and the viewid records it. Phient's loss is caused by every rounding, and there is no viewid on a rounding. Dither makes loss random instead of systematic. The paper makes loss visible. Phient has only made it smaller.

The paper's correctness criterion is one-copy serializability, and the transaction is the unit. Phient has no transactions and no criterion of that kind. The quest scores rows, not transactions, and the held-out test is a ranking, not a prepare. Section 10 read the held-out hops as prepares, but no Phient code refuses anything.

Last, the paper's claim is that its method is faster than the alternatives, by message count, and its 2012 revision by Liskov and Cowling says, per the abstract reached, that it handles failures "in which nodes crash" and adds a reconfiguration protocol. A secondary account of that paper reports it saying that "VR is a replication protocol rather than a consensus protocol" and that VR "did not require disk I/O." Phient has no message count and no disk. The engine is allocation and bandwidth bound at 1 MB op in 2–5 ms, and nothing in that number says what the paper's numbers say. The comparisons are not comparable.

## 14. The person and the moment

The paper is by Brian M. Oki and Barbara H. Liskov of the MIT Laboratory for Computer Science, and it appeared in the proceedings of the ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing in 1988, pages 8 to 17. The paper cites Oki's dissertation as reference 32, "Viewstamped Replication," and the 2010 Springer chapter by Liskov gives that dissertation as MIT-LCS-TR-423, May 1988. The paper says the nested-transaction version of the algorithm is in the dissertation and is based on Argus.

Argus is the moment. The paper's model of modules at single nodes communicating by remote procedure calls, of transactions with two-phase commit, and of one-copy serializability as the criterion is the Argus model, and the paper's reference to Liskov and Scheifler 1983, "Guardians and actions," is the source of it. Viewstamped replication answers a question Argus raised: a guardian at one node is as available as that node. The paper's acknowledgments name readers of drafts, "Sanjay Ghemawat, Dave Gifford, Bob Gruber, Deborah Hwang, Elliot Kolodner, Gary Leavens, Sharon Perl, Liuba Shrira, and Bill Weihl."

What came after is on the record. Liskov's 2010 chapter, "From Viewstamped Replication to Byzantine Fault Tolerance," in the Springer volume Replication, describes two protocols from the Programming Methodology group: viewstamped replication, "developed in the 1980s," enabling "a group of replicas to continue to provide service in spite of a certain number of crashes among them," and a second that extends it to Byzantine failures. In July 2012 Liskov and James Cowling issued "Viewstamped Replication Revisited," MIT-CSAIL-TR-2012-021, whose abstract describes a replication technique for nodes that crash, covering client requests, group reorganization after a replica fails, rejoining, optimizations, and "a protocol for handling reconfigurations that can change both the group membership and the number of failures the group is able to handle." A secondary account of the revisit reports that it strips the transactions, replaces the viewstamp with a view number and an op number, uses 2f + 1 replicas to survive f faults, and picks the primary by rotating "in round-robin order of ascending IP addresses." I could not read the revisit itself and do not report more of it.

Liskov received the Turing Award in 2008. She was at MITRE and then MIT,. The 1988 paper is twenty years after the 1968 thesis and twenty years before the award, and it is the same method of decision: a group chooses by comparison, keeps what is better, refutes what leads nowhere, and does not pretend that what was lost was kept.

## 15. What remains

The experiment in section 12 is the first item. Majority-gated commit against the dithered trainer, on the quest, with drop counts logged per epoch.

A viewstamp on a rounding. The paper's loss is detectable because a view change has an identity. Phient's rounding has none. A version counter per relation, incremented at each rounding that changes the byte and recorded with the count, would let a later pass ask whether a nudge it wants to make was already lost once. That is the history check, and Phient has no history.

A majority of channels. Section 6 said the paper's requirement that a view be a majority has no measured analogue. The experiment is cheap: withhold a fraction of a word's channels at read time and measure the operator's MRR as the fraction grows. If the answer survives a minority withheld and fails at a majority, the paper's invariant holds for words. If it fails at once, a word has no redundancy and the analogy in section 6 is only a shape.

A primary. The paper picks the cohort with the largest viewstamp and keeps the old primary if possible. Phient reads all channels at once and has no first. Whether the operator would do better reading one channel first, and which, and whether that choice should be sticky across retraining, is unmeasured.

The count as a predictor. The count on each relation was read in section 10 as the backup's record. No one has checked whether it predicts anything: whether high-count relations are the ones that survive rounding, or the ones that score better above zero.

The three operators as signals. Every operator returns a number, and section 9 read the quest's flat table as a table of refusals. The paper refuses. If loss above the margin returned a refusal instead of a number, the trainer could count refusals per epoch, and the count would be the paper's lost work. docs/86 lists exposing better, worse and loss as the trainer's three names; this is the same item.

The Phi-4 quest. The hexagram table is flat on the synthetic oracle and the facts file says the table is there for a corpus that is not flat. Until that run is made, every operator number in this paper is a number about a flat quest.

The revisit. The 2012 paper was reached only by abstract and secondary account. Its reconfiguration protocol, which changes group membership and the number of tolerable failures, is the part most likely to bear on a lexicon whose channel count is 32 to 64 and not fixed. It should be read.
